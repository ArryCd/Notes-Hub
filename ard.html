Chapter 1

Introduction to Nuclear Physics

Why "Nuclear Physics = Unclear Physics"?

Nuclear physics, as Prof. ARD quips, might as well be called unclear physics — for two fundamental reasons:

Many-Body System
A nucleus contains $Z$ protons and $N$ neutrons, all interacting simultaneously via the nuclear force. Unlike the hydrogen atom (an exact two-body problem), the nuclear many-body problem has no closed-form solution for $A \geq 3$.
Force Not Fully Known
The nucleon–nucleon interaction is an effective residual force from the underlying quark–gluon dynamics of QCD. Unlike gravity or electromagnetism, it has no simple analytic form, and even today its detailed structure is an area of active research.

Starting of Nuclear Physics

Interestingly, nuclear physics began even before the nucleus was discovered. In 1896, Henri Becquerel discovered radioactivity while experimenting with uranium. He observed that uranium spontaneously emitted three types of radiation:

Alpha (α)
$^4_2$He
Helium-4 nucleus, charge +2e
Beta (β)
e⁻ or e⁺
Electron or positron from weak decay
Gamma (γ)
Photon
High-energy electromagnetic wave

The need to understand where these energetic projectiles originated led directly to scattering experiments — and the discovery of the nucleus itself.

Rutherford's Gold Foil Experiment (1911)

The prevailing model before 1911 was Thomson's Plum-Pudding Model: positive charge is uniformly distributed over a sphere of radius equal to the atomic radius ($\sim 10^{-10}$ m), with electrons embedded like plums in a pudding. From this model, only very small angular deviations of α-particles were expected.

Fig 1.1 — Geiger and Marsden's experimental setup. The α-source fires particles at a thin gold foil; the scattered particles hit a circular fluorescent screen.

Outcome of the Experiment

The results were shocking:

Rutherford's Astonishment
"It was quite the most incredible event that ever happened to me in my life. It was as incredible as if you had fired a 15-inch shell at a piece of tissue paper and it came back and hit you."

The key observations were: (1) Most α-particles passed through undeviated. (2) A few were deflected through large angles. (3) A very small number actually rebounded back.

Conclusion: The Nuclear Atom

Rutherford's interpretation: Most of the atom is empty space. All the positive charge and essentially all the mass is concentrated in an extremely tiny, dense region — the nucleus — of radius $\sim 10^{-15}$ m = 1 fm. Electrons orbit around this central nucleus.

Scales of the Atomic Nucleus

ObjectLength ScaleEnergy ScaleUnit
Atom$\sim 10^{-10}$ meVÅ (Angstrom)
Nucleus$\sim 10^{-15}$ mMeVfm (femtometer)
Why MeV at the nuclear scale?
The Heisenberg uncertainty principle gives $\Delta x \cdot \Delta p \geq \hbar$. Confining a nucleon to $\Delta x \sim 1$ fm gives $\Delta p \sim \hbar/\text{fm}$, which corresponds to a kinetic energy $\sim p^2/2m \sim 20$ MeV. This is why nuclear physics uses MeV as its natural energy unit — a factor of $10^6$ larger than atomic physics.

Nuclear Properties

Nuclear Size

A variety of scattering experiments using different projectiles have been used to determine nuclear dimensions. Fast electrons (which interact only via the electromagnetic force) are ideal for measuring the charge distribution, while neutrons (interacting only via the nuclear force) probe the matter distribution. In every case, experiments show that the volume of a nucleus is directly proportional to $A$ — the number of nucleons.

We model the nucleus as a sphere of radius $R$ that scales with mass number as:

Nuclear Radius Formula
$$R = R_0 \, A^{1/3}$$
where $R_0 \approx 1.2$ fm (range: 1.1–1.5 fm) is experimentally determined
Physical Meaning of R = R₀A^(1/3)
Since $R \propto A^{1/3}$, we get $R^3 \propto A$, so the volume $V = \frac{4}{3}\pi R^3 \propto A$. This means each nucleon occupies roughly the same volume regardless of which nucleus it's in — a profound statement about the nuclear force (saturation property).
Nuclei do NOT have sharp boundaries
The radius $R$ represents only an effective nuclear size. The nuclear density $\rho(r)$ has a diffuse surface that falls off over a distance of about 2–3 fm (the "skin thickness"). Charge density and mass density follow each other closely though not exactly.
Fig 2.1 — Nuclear charge/mass density profile. The density is approximately constant inside and falls smoothly to zero over ~2 fm at the surface.

Nuclear Density

Treating the nucleus as a uniform sphere, we can derive the nuclear density explicitly:

01
$V = \tfrac{4}{3}\pi R^3 = \tfrac{4}{3}\pi R_0^3 A$
Volume of sphere, substituting $R = R_0 A^{1/3}$
02
$M \approx A \cdot u$ where $u = 1.66 \times 10^{-27}$ kg
Total nuclear mass in atomic mass units (mass of p ≈ mass of n ≈ u)
03
$\rho_\text{nuc} = \dfrac{M}{V} \approx \dfrac{A \cdot u}{\frac{4}{3}\pi R_0^3 A} = \dfrac{3u}{4\pi R_0^3}$
The factors of A cancel — density is independent of which nucleus!
04
$\rho_\text{nuc} \approx 0.138 \; \text{u/fm}^3 \approx 2.3 \times 10^{17} \; \text{kg/m}^3$
Numerical result using $R_0 = 1.2$ fm
Astonishing Fact
$\rho_\text{nuc} \approx 10^{14} \times \rho_\text{water}$. Nuclear matter is 100 trillion times denser than water. This is comparable to the density of a neutron star — essentially a nucleus the size of a city! A teaspoon of nuclear matter would weigh ~1 billion tonnes.

Nuclear Binding Energy

The binding energy $E_B$ of a nucleus is the energy that must be supplied to completely separate all nucleons from each other. Equivalently, it is the magnitude of the energy by which the nucleons are bound together within the nucleus.

Definition
$$E_B = E_0 - E_N$$
$E_0$ = total rest energy of the separated nucleons  |  $E_N$ = rest energy of the bound nucleus

Derivation Using Atomic Masses

For a nucleus $^A_Z X$ with $N = A - Z$ neutrons:

01
$E_0 = (Z \cdot m_p + N \cdot m_n)c^2$
Rest energy of $Z$ free protons and $N$ free neutrons
02
$E_N = m_N c^2$   (where $m_N$ = nuclear mass)
Rest energy of the actual bound nucleus
03
$E_B = (Zm_p + Nm_n - m_N)c^2 = \Delta m \cdot c^2$
$\Delta m$ is the mass defect — nucleus weighs less than its constituents!
04
$= (Zm_p + Zm_e + Nm_n)c^2 - (m_N + Zm_e)c^2$
Add and subtract $Zm_e$ (electron masses)
05
$$\boxed{E_B = \bigl(Z M_H + N m_n - {}^A_Z M\bigr)c^2}$$
Final result in terms of atomic masses ($M_H$ = atomic mass of $^1$H, $^A_Z M$ = atomic mass of nucleus $X$). This is the tabulated form used in practice.
Why Atomic Masses?
Nuclear masses $m_N$ are hard to measure directly. What's tabulated are atomic masses $M(A,Z)$ which include the electron masses. When we write $E_B$ in terms of $M_H$ and $M(A,Z)$, the electron binding energies partially cancel (we neglect their small contribution), leaving the clean formula above.

The Binding Energy Curve — $E_B/A$ vs. $A$

The most important graph in all of nuclear physics. It plots the average binding energy per nucleon $E_B/A$ against mass number $A$.

Fig 2.2 — Binding energy per nucleon vs. mass number. The peak near A ≈ 60 defines the most stable nuclei. Fusion releases energy for A < 60; fission releases energy for A > 60.
Key Features
• $E_B/A$ rises sharply for light nuclei (up to He-4)
• Approximately constant at ~7–9 MeV for most nuclei
• Maximum at $^{62}$Ni (8.795 MeV/nucleon) — most tightly bound
• Gradual decrease for heavy nuclei (A > 100)
Fusion & Fission
Fusion: Light nuclei (A < 60) combine → product has higher $E_B/A$ → energy released

Fission: Heavy nucleus (A > 200) splits → fragments have higher $E_B/A$ → energy released

Both processes move toward the stability peak.

Nuclear Spin

Like the electron, protons and neutrons are spin-$\frac{1}{2}$ fermions. Each nucleon has both intrinsic spin angular momentum and orbital angular momentum. The total angular momentum of the nucleus $\vec{I}$ is the vector sum:

Nuclear Spin (Total Angular Momentum)
$$\vec{I} = \vec{L} + \vec{S}$$
$\vec{L} = \sum_i \vec{\ell}_i$ (sum of orbital angular momenta of all nucleons)  |  $\vec{S} = \sum_i \vec{s}_i$ (sum of spins)
$s_i = \frac{1}{2}$ for each nucleon  |  $\ell_i = 0, 1, 2, \ldots$ for each nucleon

The magnitude of $\vec{I}$ is $\sqrt{I(I+1)}\,\hbar$ where $I$ is the nuclear spin quantum number — the maximum projection along any chosen axis.

Empirical Rules for Nuclear Spin

Odd A nucleus (Fermion)
Obeys Fermi-Dirac statistics. Spin is half-integer:
$I = \frac{1}{2}, \frac{3}{2}, \frac{5}{2}, \ldots$ (max $\approx \frac{9}{2}$)
Even A nucleus (Boson)
Obeys Bose-Einstein statistics. Spin is integer:
$I = 0, 1, 2, \ldots$
Even-Even Nucleus: I = 0
For all even-$Z$, even-$N$ nuclei in the ground state, $I = 0$ without exception. This shows that nucleons pair up with equal and oppositely directed angular momenta inside the nucleus. Nucleons form a core of paired protons and paired neutrons (each pair contributing zero total angular momentum). The few unpaired nucleons outside the core determine the nuclear spin.

Nuclear Magnetic Moment

An electron in a circular orbit has a magnetic (dipole) moment of 1 Bohr magneton:

Bohr Magneton vs. Nuclear Magneton
$$\mu_B = \frac{e\hbar}{2m_e} \approx 9.27 \times 10^{-24} \text{ J/T}$$ $$\mu_N = \frac{e\hbar}{2m_p} \approx 5.05 \times 10^{-27} \text{ J/T}$$
$\mu_B/\mu_N \approx 1836$ — nuclear effects are ~1800× smaller than atomic magnetic effects

The net magnetic moment of a nucleus depends on its spin $I$:

$$\vec{\mu} = g\,\mu_N \vec{I}$$
$g$ = gyromagnetic ratio (different for each nucleus)
Strange Anomaly!
If the proton were a simple point charge, its magnetic moment should be exactly $1\,\mu_N$. But experiments give:
• Proton: $\mu_p = +2.79\,\mu_N$ (not $1\,\mu_N$!) — much larger than expected
• Neutron: $\mu_n = -1.91\,\mu_N$ (not $0$!) — has a magnetic moment despite being electrically neutral!

These anomalies are explained by the internal charge structure of nucleons — they are made of quarks (up and down quarks with fractional charges), not elementary point particles.

Parity of the Nucleus

Parity is a purely quantum mechanical concept with no classical analogue. It describes a kind of spatial symmetry — what happens to the wavefunction under a reflection through the origin (parity transformation: $\mathbf{r} \to -\mathbf{r}$).

If $\psi(\mathbf{r})$ is the spatial part of the nuclear wavefunction:

Parity Eigenstates
$\psi(-x,-y,-z) = +\psi(x,y,z)$ → even (+) parity, $\pi = +1$
$\psi(-x,-y,-z) = -\psi(x,y,z)$ → odd (−) parity, $\pi = -1$

For a single nucleon with orbital quantum number $\ell$, the parity of the spherical harmonic $Y_\ell^m$ under $\mathbf{r} \to -\mathbf{r}$ gives $(-1)^\ell$. For the whole nucleus:

Nuclear Parity
$$\pi = (-1)^L \quad \text{where } L = \sum_i \ell_i$$
Even $L$ → even parity ($+$)  |  Odd $L$ → odd parity ($-$)
Spin-Parity Notation
The spin and parity of a nuclear state are combined into the notation $I^\pi$, e.g., $0^+$, $\frac{1}{2}^-$, $3^+$, etc. For all even-even nuclei in the ground state: $I^\pi = 0^+$. This is one of the most robustly verified facts in nuclear physics.

The Nuclear Force

The attractive force that binds protons and neutrons together inside the nucleus — despite the strong Coulomb repulsion between protons — is known as the nuclear force (or residual strong force). It was historically considered the strongest force in nature; today we understand it as a residual effect of the quark–gluon interaction (the colour force or strong force) described by QCD.

PROPERTY 1

Charge Independent

The nuclear force acts equally between n–n, p–p, and n–p pairs. It does not distinguish between protons and neutrons (isospin symmetry: proton and neutron are two states of the same particle).

PROPERTY 2

Short Range

Strongly attractive at distances ~1 fm. Negligible beyond 2–3 fm. At very short distances (<0.7 fm), it becomes strongly repulsive — this "hard core" gives nuclei their finite size and prevents gravitational collapse. The exact form is still not fully known.

PROPERTY 3

Saturates

A nucleon interacts only with its few nearest neighbours, not with all other nucleons in the nucleus. This is why $E_B/A$ is approximately constant for large nuclei. (Coulomb and gravitational forces do NOT show saturation.)

PROPERTY 4

Spin Dependent

The nuclear force favours binding of pairs with opposite spins (↑↓) over parallel spins (↑↑). This is why spin-singlet pairs are more tightly bound — the deuteron exists only in a spin-triplet state (no bound spin-singlet n-p state).

Fig 2.3 — Nuclear force vs. separation. Repulsive core below ~0.7 fm; strong attraction near 1 fm; negligible beyond ~2.5 fm. The exact form of this curve is still not known from first principles.

Alpha Radioactivity

Radioactivity raises deep questions: What is the lifetime of a particular radio-nucleus? How do we distinguish between different radio-nuclides? The key insight is that different nuclides have different decay constants $\lambda$ — and hence different half-lives $t_{1/2} = \ln 2 / \lambda$.

Radioactive Decay Law
$N(t) = N_0 e^{-\lambda t}$ where $\lambda$ is the disintegration constant. Different $\lambda$ values give different half-lives — this is how we fingerprint each radioactive species. The half-life $t_{1/2} = \ln 2/\lambda$ ranges from microseconds ($^{212}$Po) to billions of years ($^{238}$U).

What is Alpha? — Rutherford & Royds Experiment

Alpha particles were identified as $^4_2$He nuclei by Rutherford and Royds (1909) through a beautiful spectroscopic experiment:

1
Setup
A thin-walled glass bulb $\alpha$ contains $^{222}$Rn. Surrounded by a thick-walled tube $T$ with a mercury reservoir $D$. Electrodes $P$ and $Q$ in capillary tube $E$. Region between $\alpha$ and $E$ is evacuated.
2
Waiting Period
After a few days, α-particles from Rn decay pass through the thin wall of bulb $\alpha$ and collect as gas in the evacuated space. Mercury is raised to compress this gas into $E$.
3
Spectroscopic Analysis
An electrical discharge is struck between $P$ and $Q$. Light emitted by the discharge is analysed spectroscopically → gives the line spectrum of helium.
4
Control Experiment
A blank experiment with He gas in $\alpha$ — no He spectrum is observed, confirming the He came from the decay product, not contamination.
Conclusion
Alpha particles are helium-4 nuclei ($^4_2$He, charge +2e).

Q-Value & Energetics of Alpha Decay

Why Specifically ⁴He?

Alpha emission arises from Coulomb repulsion. As $Z$ increases, repulsion grows as $Z^2$, but nuclear binding only grows as $A$ (due to saturation). So for heavy nuclei, emission of a charged fragment becomes energetically favourable.

But why $^4$He and not $^1$H, $^2$H, or $^3$He? The answer lies in the disintegration energy $Q$:

Disintegration Energy
$$Q = (m_X - m_Y - m_y)c^2$$
For spontaneous decay, $Q$ must be positive

For $^{232}$U as parent nucleus:

Particle EmittedQ (MeV)Possible?
Neutron n−7.15No
Proton $^1$H−6.05No
$^3$He−9.6No
$^4$He (α)+5.38✓ Yes!
$^7$Li−1.83No
Why only α?
The exceptionally high binding energy of $^4$He ($E_B/A \approx 7.07$ MeV, much higher than $^3$He or triton) makes it uniquely stable. The 2p+2n system is a "magic" closed-shell configuration with enhanced stability.
Not just energetics
For nuclei with A > 190, α-decay is energetically possible, but only about half actually show observable α-activity. The reason: the half-life must be physically observable ($t_{1/2} > 10^{-6}$ y). This requires the theory of quantum tunneling.

Theoretical Derivation of Alpha Particle Energy

Alpha Decay Reaction
$^A_Z X \rightarrow ^{A-4}_{Z-2} Y + \alpha$
1
$Q = (M_x - M_y - M_\alpha)c^2$
Q-value in terms of atomic masses
2
Momentum conservation (X at rest): $\quad 0 = M_\alpha v_\alpha - M_Y v_Y \quad \Rightarrow \quad v_Y = \frac{M_\alpha}{M_Y} v_\alpha$
Linear momentum conservation in CM frame
3
$Q = \tfrac{1}{2}M_Y v_Y^2 + \tfrac{1}{2}M_\alpha v_\alpha^2 = T_\alpha\!\left(1 + \tfrac{M_\alpha}{M_Y}\right)$
Substituting $v_Y$ and collecting terms
4
$$\boxed{T_\alpha = \frac{M_Y}{M_Y + M_\alpha}\,Q \qquad T_Y = \frac{M_\alpha}{M_Y + M_\alpha}\,Q}$$
Since $M_Y \gg M_\alpha$, almost all of Q goes to the α-particle. Also $T_Y/T_\alpha = M_\alpha/M_Y \ll 1$.
Key Result
$T_\alpha$ is fixed for a particular decay — so α-particles are strictly monoenergetic. This is a distinguishing feature of α-decay vs. β-decay.

Energy or Velocity of Alpha Particles

The velocity of α-particles is measured using a magnetic deflection apparatus: an evacuated box in a transverse magnetic field $B$. Particles travel semicircular paths of radius $\rho$:

Magnetic Deflection
$$\frac{mv^2}{\rho} = Bqv \quad \Rightarrow \quad v = \frac{Bq\rho}{m}$$
For natural radionuclei: $v \approx 10^7$ m/s, corresponding to K.E. of 4–10 MeV

Alpha Energy Spectrum

Many elements emit only a single line (one value of $\rho$) → strictly monoenergetic α-particles. Some emit two or more closely spaced lines → discrete multiline spectrum. This fine structure arises from the existence of discrete nuclear energy levels in the daughter nucleus.

Fig 3.1 — Alpha energy spectrum showing main peak and fine structure. Multiple lines arise from decays to different energy levels of the daughter nucleus Y.

Range of Alpha Particles

The range of an α-particle is the ionisation path-length — the total distance it travels in a medium before losing all its kinetic energy. It depends on: (i) initial K.E., (ii) chance of collision, (iii) ionisation potential of the medium; and for a gas, also on temperature and pressure.

In gas (air)
~cm
A few centimetres for natural α-emitters
In solid/liquid
~10⁻³ mm
Due to high density, much shorter path
Geiger's Law
$R = bE^{3/2}$
$b = 0.315$, R in cm, E in MeV

Geiger showed empirically for monoenergetic α-particles of velocity $v$: $R \propto v^3 \propto E^{3/2}$. Since range is expressed as mass per unit area (kg/m²) for comparing different materials, the range is of the same order of magnitude in different materials.

Straggling of Range

α-particles with the same initial energy do not have exactly the same range — there is a small spread around the mean value $\bar{R}$. This is called straggling. At $\bar{R}$, the intensity falls to half its initial value; 30% of particles have range > $\bar{R}$ and 50% have range < $\bar{R}$. Causes: (i) statistical fluctuation in number of collisions, (ii) statistical fluctuation in energy loss per collision.

Geiger-Nuttal Law (1911)

Geiger and Nuttal discovered an empirical relationship between the range $R$ of α-particles and the disintegration constant $\lambda$ of the naturally α-active substances:

Geiger-Nuttal Law
$$\ln \lambda = A + B\ln R \qquad \Leftrightarrow \qquad \ln t_{1/2} = C - D\ln E$$
$A$, $B$, $C$, $D$ are constants. $B$ is the same for all three natural radioactive series; $A$ differs between series.
Physical Implication
α-particles with larger range (higher energy) have smaller half-life (larger $\lambda$). This is a remarkable finding: a small change in energy leads to an enormous change in half-life — spanning 20 orders of magnitude! For example, $^{212}$Po ($E_\alpha = 8.78$ MeV) has $t_{1/2} = 3 \times 10^{-7}$ s, while $^{238}$U ($E_\alpha = 4.27$ MeV) has $t_{1/2} = 4.5 \times 10^9$ years. This extreme sensitivity is explained by Gamow's tunnelling theory.

Theory of Alpha Decay — Gamow's Theory (1928)

Classically, α-decay is completely inexplicable. α-particles emitted from natural radionuclei have energies of 4–10 MeV — far below the height of the Coulomb barrier, which is ~35 MeV for $^{238}$U. The problem was solved in 1928 by Gamow, Condon, and Gurney using the newly developed Schrödinger wave equation: the α-particle escapes by quantum tunnelling.

Fig 3.2 — Gamow's potential for α-decay. The α-particle is trapped in the nuclear well by the Coulomb barrier. Classically, escape is impossible since $E_\alpha \ll V_\text{barrier}$. Quantum mechanics allows tunnelling.

The barrier height at the nuclear surface $r_0$ is:

$$V_\text{barrier} = \frac{2(Z-2)e^2}{r_0} \approx \begin{cases} 35.6 \text{ MeV} & \text{for } ^{238}\text{U} \\ 34 \text{ MeV} & \text{for } ^{222}\text{Rn} \end{cases}$$

Since $E_\alpha \approx 4\text{–}10$ MeV $\ll V_\text{barrier} \approx 34\text{–}36$ MeV, classically the α-particle can never escape. But quantum mechanically it can tunnel through!

Optical Analogy for Tunnelling
Consider total internal reflection at a glass–air interface. If a second glass surface is brought within a few wavelengths of the first, light leaks through the air gap (frustrated total internal reflection). Similarly, the quantum mechanical α-particle wave leaks through the Coulomb barrier if the barrier is not too thick.

Barrier Penetration — Quantum Mechanical Treatment

Consider a simplified rectangular barrier: height $V_0$, width $a$. An α-particle with energy $E < V_0$ approaches along the $x$-axis. We divide space into three regions:

Regions
Region I:   $x < 0$,   $V = 0$
Region II:   $0 \leq x \leq a$,   $V = V_0$
Region III:   $x > a$,   $V = 0$

Schrödinger Equations

Time-independent Schrödinger Equation in each region
$$\text{I}: \quad -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{d^2\psi_1}{dx^2} = E\psi_1 \quad \Rightarrow \quad \psi_1'' + \alpha^2\psi_1 = 0$$ $$\text{II}: \quad -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{d^2\psi_2}{dx^2} + V_0\psi_2 = E\psi_2 \quad \Rightarrow \quad \psi_2'' - \beta^2\psi_2 = 0$$ $$\text{III}: \quad -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{d^2\psi_3}{dx^2} = E\psi_3 \quad \Rightarrow \quad \psi_3'' + \alpha^2\psi_3 = 0$$
where $\alpha^2 = \dfrac{2mE}{\hbar^2}$ and $\beta^2 = \dfrac{2m(V_0 - E)}{\hbar^2}$

Solutions

$$\psi_1 = Ae^{i\alpha x} + Be^{-i\alpha x} \quad \psi_2 = De^{-\beta x} + Fe^{\beta x} \quad \psi_3 = Ce^{i\alpha x}$$
$\psi_\text{in} = Ae^{i\alpha x}$ (incident), $\psi_\text{ref} = Be^{-i\alpha x}$ (reflected), $\psi_\text{tr} = Ce^{i\alpha x}$ (transmitted)

Transmission Coefficient

Applying boundary conditions ($\psi$ and $\psi'$ continuous at $x=0$ and $x=a$) gives 4 equations. The transmission coefficient is:

1
$T = \dfrac{j_\text{tr}}{j_\text{in}} = \dfrac{|C|^2}{|A|^2} = \dfrac{4\beta^2\alpha^2}{(\beta^2 - \alpha^2)^2\sinh^2\!\beta a + 4\beta^2\alpha^2\cosh^2\!\beta a}$
Exact result from solving the 4 boundary condition equations
2
$T = \dfrac{1}{1 + \dfrac{V_0^2}{4E(V_0-E)}\sinh^2\!\beta a}$
Simplified form after substituting $\alpha$ and $\beta$
3
For $\beta a \gg 1$:   $\sinh\beta a \approx e^{\beta a}/2$,   so:
Limit of thick/high barrier (realistic nuclear case)
4
$$\boxed{T \approx \frac{16E(V_0-E)}{V_0^2}\,e^{-2\beta a}}$$
Even with $E \ll V_0$, $T \neq 0$! The exponential factor $e^{-2\beta a}$ quantifies the tunnelling probability.
Key Insight
Both $a$ (barrier width) and $E$ (particle energy) appear in the exponent of $T$. The transmission probability is therefore exquisitely sensitive to these parameters — a 20% increase in barrier width can change $T$ by a factor of $10^7$! This explains the enormous range of α half-lives ($10^{-7}$ s to $10^{10}$ y) for a small variation in energy (~5–9 MeV).

Disintegration Constant from Gamow Theory

The α-particle bounces back and forth inside the nucleus with velocity $v_\text{in}$. Each time it hits the barrier, it has a probability $T$ of tunnelling through.

1
$n = \dfrac{v_\text{in}}{2r_0}$   collisions per second
Number of times the α-particle hits the barrier per unit time
2
$\lambda = n \cdot T = \dfrac{v_\text{in}}{2r_0} \cdot \dfrac{16E(V_0-E)}{V_0^2}\,e^{-2\beta a}$
Disintegration constant = collision frequency × transmission coefficient
3
Mean lifetime: $\quad \tau_m = \dfrac{1}{\lambda} = \dfrac{1}{nT}$
Inverse of the decay constant
Numerical Illustration
For typical parameters: $a = 2\times10^{-14}$ m, $V_0 = 15$ MeV, $E = 5$ MeV, $m = 4 \times 1.66 \times 10^{-27}$ kg:
$\beta = 1.388 \times 10^{15}$ m$^{-1}$,   $T \approx 2.55 \times 10^{-24}$
$n = 1.7\times10^7 / (2\times10^{-14}) \approx 8.5\times10^{20}$ s$^{-1}$
$\lambda = nT \approx 2.17\times10^{-3}$ s$^{-1}$   →   $\tau_m \approx 461$ s
If barrier width increases by 20%: $\tau_m \approx 3\times10^7$ s $\approx 0.37$ yr — an increase of $6.6\times10^4$ times!

Beta Decay

Beta decay was initially observed by Madame Curie, who showed that β-particles are negatively charged. Becquerel measured $e/m$ and confirmed they are electrons ($\beta^-$). Later, positrons ($\beta^+$) were also found to be emitted. All beta decay processes occur due to the weak nuclear force.

Types of Beta Decay & Energetics

① β⁻ Decay (Negatron Emission)

Beta-Minus Decay
$^A_Z X \rightarrow ^A_{Z+1} Y + \beta^- + \bar\nu_e$   [n → p + e⁻]
Nucleus gains a proton, loses a neutron. Daughter has $Z+1$.
Q-value for β⁻ (in atomic masses)
$$Q_{\beta^-} = [M(A,Z) - M(A,Z+1)]c^2$$
Spontaneous if $M(A,Z) > M(A,Z+1)$

② β⁺ Decay (Positron Emission)

Beta-Plus Decay
$^A_Z X \rightarrow ^A_{Z-1} Y + \beta^+ + \nu_e$   [p → n + e⁺]
Q-value for β⁺ (in atomic masses)
$$Q_{\beta^+} = [M(A,Z) - M(A,Z-1) - 2m_e]c^2$$
Spontaneous if $M(A,Z) > M(A,Z-1) + 2m_e$. The threshold is $2m_e c^2 = 1.022$ MeV higher than β⁻.

③ Electron Capture (EC)

Electron Capture
$^A_Z X + e^- \rightarrow ^A_{Z-1} Y + \nu_e$   [p + e⁻ → n]
Nucleus absorbs one of its own orbital electrons (usually K-shell). Competes with β⁺.
$$Q_\text{EC} = [M(A,Z) - M(A,Z-1)]c^2 - B_e$$
$B_e$ = binding energy of captured electron. For light atoms, $B_e$ is very small and can be neglected.
Note
Both β⁺ emission and EC lead to the same daughter nucleus. If $M(A,Z) > M(A,Z-1) + 2m_e$, both are possible; if $M(A,Z-1) + 2m_e > M(A,Z) > M(A,Z-1)$, only EC can occur.

The Continuous Beta Spectrum

In contrast to α-decay (discrete, monoenergetic), the kinetic energy distribution of β-particles is continuous, ranging from 0 to a maximum endpoint $E_\text{max} \approx Q$. This continuous distribution is called the beta spectrum.

Fig 4.1 — Continuous beta spectrum. The distribution peaks at intermediate energies and ends at E_max ≈ Q.
Crisis: Three Conservation Violations!
If β-decay were simply $n \to p + e^-$ (two-body decay), then by momentum conservation, $E_e$ should be fixed — giving a discrete line spectrum. The continuous spectrum implies the electron does NOT carry all the energy Q. This leads to apparent violations of:

① Energy conservation: $(E_K)_\text{max} \approx Q$ but generally $E_K \ll Q$. Where does the missing energy go?
② Angular momentum (spin): $n \to p + e^-$ is $\frac{1}{2} \to \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{2}$ — spin angular momentum is NOT conserved (half-integer cannot equal two half-integers added).
③ Linear momentum: The emitted electron does not always travel opposite to the recoiling nucleus — required by two-body conservation, but not observed.

Pauli's Neutrino Hypothesis (1927)

To resolve these crises, Wolfgang Pauli proposed in 1927 one of the most daring postulates in physics — the neutrino hypothesis. He conjectured that in β-decay, a second new light particle (called the neutrino, named by Fermi) is simultaneously emitted with the electron.

Properties of the Neutrino

How the Neutrino Resolves All Three Crises

Correct Beta Decay Equations
$$n \to p + e^- + \bar\nu_e \quad \text{(β⁻ decay)}$$ $$p \to n + e^+ + \nu_e \quad \text{(β⁺ decay)}$$
The antineutrino $\bar\nu_e$ in β⁻ and neutrino $\nu_e$ in β⁺ are required by lepton number conservation.
Energy Conservation ✓
Q is shared continuously between the electron and neutrino: $Q \approx E_K + E_\nu$. Since $E_\nu$ varies, $E_K$ has a continuous distribution up to $E_\text{max} \approx Q$. Missing energy is carried away by the (undetected) neutrino!
Spin & Momentum ✓
With three particles: $\frac{1}{2} \to \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{2}$ — spin is conserved (three spin-½ particles can sum to ½). Linear momentum: three-body kinematics means $e^-$ and nucleus need not be exactly back-to-back.
The Most Elusive Particle
The neutrino's last property — extreme weakness of interaction — makes it notoriously difficult to detect. Reines and Cowan ultimately detected it in 1956, 29 years after Pauli proposed it, using inverse beta decay: $p + \bar\nu_e \to n + e^+$ near a nuclear reactor.

Lepton Number Conservation

There are three lepton families: electron ($e$), muon ($\mu$), tau ($\tau$), each with its associated neutrino. Lepton number for each family must be conserved separately:

$$n \to p + e^- + \bar\nu_e \quad [L_e: \;0 = 0 + (+1) + (-1) \;\checkmark]$$ $$p \to n + e^+ + \nu_e \quad [L_e: \;0 = 0 + (-1) + (+1) \;\checkmark]$$

Fermi's Theory of Beta Decay (1934)

In 1934, Enrico Fermi developed a successful quantitative theory of β-decay based on Pauli's neutrino hypothesis. The theory is based on five key assumptions:

①
Creation Hypothesis
The electron and neutrino are created at the moment of decay by transformation of a neutron into a proton in the nucleus. They are not pre-existing.
②
Energy Sharing
Available energy $Q$ is continuously shared between the electron and neutrino, giving the continuous spectrum.
③
Electromagnetic Analogy
The β-decay process is analogous to photon emission by an excited atom, with the electron-neutrino field playing the role of the electromagnetic field.
④
Weak Interaction
The electron-neutrino field is weak — this makes time-dependent perturbation theory an excellent approximation.
⑤
Fermi Coupling Constant $g$
The interaction strength is parameterised by the Fermi coupling constant $g$ (analogous to the electric charge $e$ in electrodynamics).

Fermi Distribution for β-Particles

Using Fermi's Golden Rule (time-dependent perturbation theory), the probability of emitting a β-particle with momentum in the range $p_e$ to $p_e + dp_e$ per unit time is:

Fermi's Distribution (Allowed β-Decay)
$$P(p_e)\,dp_e = \frac{g^2}{2\pi^3 c^3 \hbar^7}|M_{if}|^2 \cdot F(Z,E_e) \cdot (E_m - E_e)^2 \cdot p_e^2\,dp_e$$
$g$ = Fermi coupling constant  |  $M_{if}$ = nuclear matrix element  |  $E_m$ = endpoint energy
$F(Z, E_e)$ = Fermi function (Coulomb correction); for low $Z$ and high $v$: $F \approx 1$
The Kurie Plot
A useful way to test Fermi's theory and extract $E_\text{max}$ is the Kurie plot: plot $\sqrt{P(p_e)/(F \cdot p_e^2)}$ vs. $E_e$. Fermi's theory predicts this to be a straight line, going to zero at $E_e = E_m$. Any deviation from linearity indicates a massive neutrino (which shifts the endpoint).

Gamma Decay

Gamma Decay — Nuclear De-excitation

Gamma decay is a nuclear de-excitation process in which an excited nucleus emits a high-energy electromagnetic photon (gamma ray). Unlike α and β decay, the nuclear composition does not change — the same nucleus simply moves to a lower energy state.

Gamma Decay Reaction
$^A_Z X^* \longrightarrow ^A_Z X + \gamma$
$^*$ denotes excited nuclear state  |  $\Delta E = E^* - E = h\nu$  |  $E_\gamma$ up to several MeV

Mean lifetimes range from $\sim 10^{-17}$ s (typical) to a few hours for isomers (long-lived excited states).

Fig 5.1 — Gamma decay modes. Left: single transition from excited to ground state. Right: cascade through intermediate levels, emitting two photons sequentially.

Key Features of Gamma Decay

Selection Rules
The multipolarity (dipole, quadrupole, etc.) of the gamma transition is determined by the angular momentum difference $\Delta I$ between nuclear levels and the parity change. Electric dipole (E1) transitions are fastest; higher multipolarities are successively slower.
Internal Conversion
Competing with gamma emission: the nucleus can transfer its excitation energy directly to an orbital electron (usually K-shell), which is then ejected with kinetic energy $E_e = \Delta E - B_e$. These produce sharp peaks ("internal conversion lines") superimposed on the continuous β-spectrum.
Connection to Alpha and Beta Decay
Often, after α or β decay, the daughter nucleus is left in an excited state. It then de-excites via γ emission. So in practice, γ-rays almost always accompany α- and β-decay. The γ-ray energies map out the nuclear energy level structure of the daughter nucleus.

Practice Questions

Nuclear Properties
Derive an expression for nuclear density and show it is independent of mass number A.
Using $R = R_0 A^{1/3}$, volume $V = \frac{4}{3}\pi R_0^3 A$. Total mass $M = Au$. So $\rho = M/V = 3u/(4\pi R_0^3)$ — the A cancels, giving a universal constant $\approx 2.3 \times 10^{17}$ kg/m³.
What is the mass defect? Write the expression for binding energy in terms of atomic masses.
Mass defect $\Delta m = ZM_H + Nm_n - {}^A_Z M$. Binding energy $E_B = \Delta m \cdot c^2 = (ZM_H + Nm_n - {}^A_ZM)c^2$. The nucleus weighs less than its constituents; this mass difference has been converted to binding energy.
Why do even-even nuclei always have spin $I = 0$ in the ground state?
Inside a nucleus, nucleons pair up with equal and oppositely directed angular momenta. For even-Z, even-N nuclei, all protons pair up (cancelling total $\vec{I}$) and all neutrons pair up similarly. The remaining spin is determined by unpaired nucleons outside the core — and in even-even nuclei, there are none.
Why does the neutron have a magnetic moment despite being electrically neutral?
The neutron has an internal charge structure — it is composed of quarks (one up quark, charge +2/3, and two down quarks, charge −1/3 each). Although the net charge is zero, the quark charges are distributed asymmetrically, giving a non-zero magnetic moment of $-1.91\mu_N$. This is direct evidence that nucleons are not elementary particles.
List and explain the four properties of the nuclear force.
(1) Charge independent: equal between n-n, p-p, n-p. (2) Short range: attractive at ~1 fm, repulsive below ~0.7 fm, negligible beyond ~2.5 fm. (3) Saturates: acts only on nearest neighbours, hence $E_B/A \approx$ const. (4) Spin dependent: favours opposite-spin pairs, hence triplet n-p (deuteron) is bound but singlet n-p is not.
Alpha Radioactivity
Why is alpha emission specifically $^4$He and not $^1$H or $^3$He?
The Q-value for emitting $^4$He from $^{232}$U is +5.38 MeV (positive — spontaneous), while for $^1$H it is −6.05 MeV and for $^3$He it is −9.6 MeV (both negative — energetically forbidden). The $^4$He nucleus is exceptionally tightly bound (closed sub-shells of 2p + 2n), making it the energetically preferred fragment.
Derive the kinetic energy of the alpha particle in terms of Q-value and masses.
From momentum conservation with parent at rest: $M_\alpha v_\alpha = M_Y v_Y$. From energy conservation: $Q = \frac{1}{2}M_Y v_Y^2 + T_\alpha$. Substituting $v_Y$: $Q = T_\alpha(1 + M_\alpha/M_Y)$, so $T_\alpha = QM_Y/(M_Y + M_\alpha)$. Since $M_Y \gg M_\alpha$, almost all of Q goes to the α-particle.
Explain the fine structure of the alpha energy spectrum. What does it tell us?
Fine structure (multiple closely-spaced lines) arises when the daughter nucleus can be left in different excited states. Each transition to a different daughter energy level gives a different Q-value, hence a different (fixed) $T_\alpha$. The fine structure thus maps out the discrete nuclear energy levels of the daughter nucleus.
Write down the Schrödinger equations for barrier penetration in three regions and state the solutions.
Region I ($V=0$): $\psi_1'' + \alpha^2\psi_1 = 0$, solution $Ae^{i\alpha x} + Be^{-i\alpha x}$. Region II ($V=V_0$): $\psi_2'' - \beta^2\psi_2 = 0$, solution $De^{-\beta x} + Fe^{\beta x}$. Region III ($V=0$): $\psi_3'' + \alpha^2\psi_3 = 0$, solution $Ce^{i\alpha x}$. Here $\alpha^2 = 2mE/\hbar^2$ and $\beta^2 = 2m(V_0-E)/\hbar^2$.
What is the Geiger-Nuttal law and what does it imply physically?
$\ln\lambda = A + B\ln R$ (or equivalently $\ln t_{1/2} = C - D\ln E$). It implies α-emitters with higher energy (larger range) have shorter half-lives. The exponential sensitivity ($T \propto e^{-2\beta a}$) means a small change in energy gives an enormous change in half-life — spanning 20 orders of magnitude from $\sim 10^{-7}$ s to $10^{10}$ yr.
Beta Decay
State three experimental difficulties with beta decay before the neutrino hypothesis.
(1) Energy: β-spectrum is continuous but Q is fixed — apparent violation of energy conservation. (2) Spin: $n \to p + e^-$ has $\frac{1}{2} \to \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{2}$ — angular momentum cannot balance. (3) Momentum: emitted electron doesn't travel exactly opposite to recoiling nucleus — violating two-body momentum conservation.
Why is Q for β⁺ decay 1.022 MeV larger than for β⁻ decay from the same parent?
In β⁺ decay, a positron (mass $m_e$) must be created. When converting nuclear masses to atomic masses, the daughter acquires $(Z-1)$ electrons. So one electron mass must be subtracted from the Q expression for the daughter's atomic mass, AND the positron mass adds another $m_e$. Total extra cost: $2m_ec^2 = 1.022$ MeV over β⁻.
Why is it impossible for electrons to be pre-existing constituents of the nucleus?
If electrons existed inside a nucleus of radius $r \sim 5\times10^{-15}$ m, by the uncertainty principle $\Delta p \geq \hbar/\Delta x \Rightarrow p \sim \hbar/r$, giving $E = pc \approx 20$ MeV. But β-electrons have energies of only 1–3 MeV — far less than required. Therefore electrons cannot be confined inside nuclei; they are created at the moment of decay.
Write the five assumptions of Fermi's theory of beta decay.
(1) Electron and neutrino are created at the moment of decay. (2) Available energy is shared continuously between them. (3) Process is analogous to photon emission, with electron-neutrino field replacing the EM field. (4) The interaction is weak — perturbation theory is valid. (5) The coupling strength is parameterised by the Fermi constant $g$.
