Setting up the Hydrogen Atom Problem

09 / 02 / 2026
Course Roadmap
This course (Atomic Physics, CO-2) covers one-electron and many-electron atoms. The mathematical prerequisites include: the Central Force Problem, the Gamma function, Legendre and Associated Legendre Polynomials (→ Spherical Harmonics), and the Confluent Hypergeometric / Associated Laguerre Polynomials. Reference: Rajput / Satya Prakash for special functions.

The Hydrogen Atom as a Two-Body Problem

The H-atom consists of one proton (mass mp, position r1) and one electron (mass me, position r2). These two charged particles interact via the Coulomb potential.

Key Problem: 6 Degrees of Freedom
The two-body system has 6 degrees of freedom (3 per particle). Since the potential depends on both positions, the Hamiltonian couples the coordinates — we cannot immediately separate the wavefunction. The full Schrödinger equation would be 6-dimensional.

The Full Hamiltonian

The Schrödinger equation for the H-atom (with external potential Veff which may or may not depend on time):

Ĥ = −ℏ²/2mp ∇²p − ℏ²/2me ∇²e − (1/4πε₀)(Ze²/r12) + Veff Full Two-body Hamiltonian

where ∇²p = ∂²/∂x₁² + ∂²/∂y₁² + ∂²/∂z₁² acts on proton coordinates and ∇²e similarly on electron coordinates.

The Coulomb Potential — Two Parts
The potential energy of the H-atom consists of:
  1. External potential Veff — e.g., from an external field (Stark effect, laser field)
  2. Coulomb interaction −Ze²/4πε₀r₁₂ — the attractive proton–electron interaction
Note: the positive–negative interaction does not contain coordinate r independently; it only depends on the relative separation r₁₂.

Important Questions — Lecture 1

Why does the H-atom Hamiltonian yield a 6-dimensional wavefunction, and why is that a problem?
The H-atom has two particles, each with 3 spatial degrees of freedom → 6 total. In quantum mechanics, the wavefunction must encode all degrees of freedom simultaneously: Ψ = Ψ(r⃗₁, r⃗₂, t). The Hamiltonian couples the proton and electron coordinates through the Coulomb potential (which depends on both r⃗₁ and r⃗₂). Therefore, Ĥ cannot be written as Ĥ₁ + Ĥ₂ with each part acting only on one particle — separation of variables would fail. The key trick is to transform to centre-of-mass (CM) coordinates and relative coordinates, which decouples the Hamiltonian into two independent parts (L-2).
What does Veff represent physically, and give two examples.
Veff is an effective external potential — any additional interaction the atom experiences from its environment. It can depend on coordinates, time, or both. Examples: (1) Electric field (Stark effect): Veff = eEr cosθ — a uniform electric field E along z shifts the energy levels and breaks degeneracy; (2) Magnetic field (Zeeman effect): Veff = μBL⃗·B⃗ — an external magnetic field B causes the energy levels to split proportionally to ml. When Veff = 0 (free atom), we recover the pure Coulomb problem.

Centre-of-Mass Separation & Symmetries

16 / 02 / 2026

Homogeneity of Space → Translational Symmetry

The Deep Reason for CM Separation
Homogeneity of space means physics is the same everywhere — shifting the entire system by a constant displacement changes nothing observable. This is a continuous symmetry, and by Noether's theorem, it implies conservation of linear momentum. Mathematically: [Ĥ, p̂] = 0 → Ĥ commutes with the total linear momentum operator. This is exactly why the centre-of-mass motion can be separated off completely.
Define CM and relative coordinates from (x₁,y₁,z₁; x₂,y₂,z₂) → (X,Y,Z, x,y,z):
R⃗CM = (mpr⃗₁ + mer⃗₂)/(mp+me),   r⃗ = r⃗₂ − r⃗₁ (relative position of electron from nucleus)
The total mass M = mp + me, reduced mass μ = mpme/(mp+me)
The transformed Hamiltonian decouples:
Ĥ = −ℏ²/2M ∇²M − ℏ²/2μ ∇²μ − Ze²/(4πε₀r) CM + Relative decomposition — Assignment: derive this from original Ĥ
Since the two parts are now decoupled (HCM + Hrel), the total wavefunction separates: ΨT(R⃗, r⃗) = ΨR(R⃗) · Ψn(r⃗), and ET = ER + En

The Centre-of-Mass Equation

−ℏ²/2M ∇²M ΨR(R⃗) = ER ΨR(R⃗)  →  ∇²M ΨR = −(2MER/ℏ²) ΨR Free particle equation — CM moves as a free particle (plane wave solution)
Physical Meaning of CM Separation
The CM moves as a free particle — translational motion with no force acting on it (since the internal Coulomb force is internal). This is the translational symmetry in action. After separating CM (3 DoF), we are left with only 3 DoF of the relative motion — the system of a particle of mass μ (reduced mass) in the Coulomb potential. This is the actual H-atom problem. The translational symmetry means linear momentum is conserved and the CM motion can be trivially solved.

Note: [Ĥ, p̂] = 0 is the mathematical statement of this symmetry (commutes with total linear momentum).

Isotropy of Space → Rotational Symmetry

Isotropy → Angular Momentum Conservation
Isotropy of space (no preferred direction) implies rotational symmetry. By Noether's theorem, this gives conservation of angular momentum. The CM, once separated, leaves us in relative coordinates with a spherically symmetric Coulomb potential. Spherical symmetry → [Ĥ, L²] = [Ĥ, Lz] = 0 → angular momentum is conserved → L² and Lz are constants of motion → can be simultaneously diagonalised with Ĥ → quantum numbers l and m are good quantum numbers.

Important Questions — Lecture 2

What is the physical significance of the reduced mass μ? How does it affect the hydrogen spectrum?
The reduced mass μ = mpme/(mp+me) encodes how the two-body problem maps onto an effective one-body problem. Physically: μ is the inertia of the relative motion. If mp → ∞ (infinitely heavy nucleus, fixed), then μ → me — recovering the familiar result. For finite nuclear mass, μ < me, shifting all energy levels slightly: En = −μZ²e⁴/(2ℏ²n²). For deuterium (D) vs hydrogen (H), the nuclear mass differs → different μ → slightly different energy levels → isotope shift in spectral lines. This is precisely why H and D spectral lines are displaced, and how isotopes were first detected spectroscopically.
Why can the CM separation be done for any two-body problem with a central potential, not just the H-atom?
The CM separation relies solely on the translational invariance of the system — the potential depending only on the separation |r⃗₁ − r⃗₂|, not on individual positions. This is true for any central force law: V(r⃗₁, r⃗₂) = V(|r⃗₁ − r⃗₂|). The transformation from (r⃗₁, r⃗₂) to (R⃗CM, r⃗) is purely a coordinate change and always works. Examples: electron-positron (positronium), proton-antiproton (protonium), muonic hydrogen (p + μ⁻) — all can be reduced to a one-body problem with the appropriate reduced mass. This is the power of the coordinate transformation.

The Radial Equation & Spherical Harmonics

23 / 02 / 2026

The Full Schrödinger Equation in Spherical Coordinates

After CM separation, the relative-motion Schrödinger equation in spherical coordinates (r, θ, φ) for a spherically symmetric potential V(r):

−ℏ²/2μ [1/r² ∂/∂r(r² ∂/∂r) + 1/(r²sinθ) ∂/∂θ(sinθ ∂/∂θ) + 1/(r²sin²θ) ∂²/∂φ²] Ψ + V(r)Ψ = EΨ SE in spherical polars — angular part = −L̂²/r²

Why L² Features? Separation of Variables

Since V = V(r) is spherically symmetric, we try Ψ(r,θ,φ) = R(r) · F(θ) · G(φ) = R(r) · Ylm(θ,φ).

The angular part of the Laplacian is proportional to L̂². Using [L², Lz] = 0 (mathematical simplicity — Lz commutes with L²), the eigenfunctions of both are the Spherical Harmonics:

L̂² Ylm(θ,φ) = l(l+1)ℏ² Ylm(θ,φ)    L̂z Ylm(θ,φ) = mℏ Ylm(θ,φ) Spherical Harmonics as simultaneous eigenfunctions of L² and Lz
Angular Momentum Conservation → Reduction to 1 DoF
Because the potential is spherically symmetric → [Ĥ, L²] = [Ĥ, Lz] = 0 → angular momentum is conserved. This is the isotropic rotational symmetry. Consequence: the θ and φ degrees of freedom separate completely from r. The entire angular structure is captured by Ylm(θ,φ), which is known exactly. The full 3D equation reduces to a 1D radial equation in r alone. From 3 DoF → 1 DoF (angular momentum conservation eliminates 2 angular co-ordinates θ and φ).

The Radial Equation

Substituting Ψ = R(r)Ylm(θ,φ) and using L̂²Y = l(l+1)ℏ²Y, the Schrödinger equation reduces to:

−ℏ²/2μ ∇² Ψ − Ze²/(4πε₀r) Ψ = EΨ

With the substitution u(r) = r R(r):

d²u/dr² + (2μ/ℏ²)[E − Veff(r)] u = 0

Veff(r) = −Ze²/(4πε₀r) + l(l+1)ℏ²/(2μr²) 1D radial equation with effective potential including centrifugal barrier

The Parity Operator

The parity operator inverts all spatial coordinates: r⃗ → −r⃗. In spherical polars: r → r, θ → π−θ, φ → φ+π.

Parity of Spherical Harmonics
Under parity: Ŷlm(θ,φ) → Ylm(π−θ, φ+π) = (−1)l Ylm(θ,φ)

Even parity (l = 0,2,4,...): (+1)   Odd parity (l = 1,3,5,...): (−1)

This has deep consequences: the electric dipole selection rule Δl = ±1 requires parity must change in a transition (since the electric dipole operator r⃗ has odd parity). States with l and l±1 have opposite parity → dipole transition allowed.
▸ Derivation: Parity operator eigenvalue equations
Parity operator P̂: r⃗ → −r⃗. This means (x,y,z) → (−x,−y,−z), or in spherical polars: (r,θ,φ) → (r, π−θ, φ+π).
Check that P̂² = 1 (applying parity twice returns to original): P̂(P̂ f(r⃗)) = P̂ f(−r⃗) = f(r⃗). So P̂² = identity operator.
Eigenvalue equation: P̂ ψ = α ψ → P̂²ψ = α²ψ = ψ → α² = 1 → α = ±1. So parity eigenvalues are ±1 (even or odd parity).
For spherical harmonics Ylm(θ,φ) = N Pl|m|(cosθ) eimφ, under θ → π−θ: cosθ → −cosθ, so Plm(cosθ) → Plm(−cosθ) = (−1)l−|m| Plm(cosθ).
Under φ → φ+π: eimφ → eim(φ+π) = eimφ · eimπ = (−1)m eimφ.
Combined parity: (−1)l−|m| × (−1)|m| = (−1)l. So P̂ Ylm = (−1)l Ylm ✓
The parity of a many-electron atom with configuration l₁, l₂, ... is the product: total parity = (−1)l₁+l₂+.... This is NOT simply (−1)l for multi-electron systems — note the notes show parity = (−1)l₁+l₂ ≠ (−1)l in general.

Important Questions — Lecture 3

Why does the centrifugal term l(l+1)ℏ²/2μr² appear in the effective potential? What is its physical origin?
In 3D, when an electron has angular momentum L⃗, there is a component of motion perpendicular to the radial direction. This transverse kinetic energy, when expressed in terms of r alone, appears as L²/2μr² = l(l+1)ℏ²/2μr². This is the quantum analogue of the classical centrifugal force (or centrifugal potential) experienced in a rotating frame. Physically: an electron with angular momentum "avoids" the nucleus — the centrifugal term pushes it away. For l = 0 (s-states), there is no centrifugal barrier, so the electron can reach r = 0 (and indeed ψ1s(r=0) ≠ 0). For l ≥ 1, the effective potential has a barrier, and the wavefunction goes to zero at r = 0 as rl.
QM says the electron "cannot state as a particle." What does this mean for the potential V(r)?
In quantum mechanics, the electron does not follow a classical trajectory. It exists as a charge distribution described by |Ψ|². The potential at a given point r depends on the charge distribution of the electron across all of space — it is an average effect. This means the effective potential V(r) experienced by an electron changes depending on its quantum state (n, l, m) because the shape of |Ψ|² changes. Therefore, V depends on the state of the electron — not just the position. In a many-electron atom, this leads to the concept of the self-consistent field (Hartree-Fock theory) where each electron moves in the average potential created by all others, and the potential must be solved self-consistently.

Solving the Radial Equation — Asymptotic Analysis & Power Series

25 / 02 / 2026

Dimensionless Variables

Introduce dimensionless variable ρ and parameter λ:

ρ = (−8μE/ℏ²)1/2 r    λ = Ze²/(4πε₀ℏ) (−μ/2E)1/2 E < 0 for bound states; −8μE/ℏ² > 0

The radial equation becomes:

[d²/dρ² − l(l+1)/ρ² + λ/ρ − 1/4] u(ρ) = 0 Standard form — the goal is to find all well-behaved solutions

Asymptotic Analysis

Two Asymptotic Regimes to Examine
Before attempting a full series solution, we must understand the behaviour at the two extremes: (i) ρ → ∞ (far from nucleus) and (ii) ρ → 0 (near nucleus). The physical solution must be well-behaved (normalisable) in both limits. This constrains the form of u(ρ) and ultimately quantises the energy.
▸ Asymptotic Limit ρ → ∞: Behaviour at far end
For ρ → ∞, the terms l(l+1)/ρ² and λ/ρ become negligible. The equation reduces to: d²u/dρ² ≈ (1/4)u
General solution: u ~ Ae+ρ/2 + Be−ρ/2
Physical requirement: u must be normalisable → u → 0 as ρ → ∞ → must choose A = 0.
∴ u ~ e−ρ/2 as ρ → ∞. This is the exponentially decaying behaviour at large r (classically forbidden region for E < 0).
▸ Asymptotic Limit ρ → 0: Behaviour near nucleus
For ρ → 0, the l(l+1)/ρ² term dominates (it's the most singular). The equation approaches: d²u/dρ² ≈ [l(l+1)/ρ²]u
Try power-law solution: u ~ ρs. Then d²u/dρ² ~ s(s−1)ρs−2.
Substituting: s(s−1)ρs−2 = l(l+1)ρs−2 → s(s−1) = l(l+1). This is a quadratic: s = l+1 or s = −l.
The solution s = −l gives u ~ ρ−l which diverges at ρ → 0 and is not admissible (not normalisable for l ≥ 1, and |Ψ|² would be singular).
∴ u ~ ρl+1 as ρ → 0. This means R(r) = u/r ~ rl → the wavefunction vanishes at the nucleus for l ≥ 1, goes to a constant for l = 0.
Summary: Boundary Conditions Fixed the Form
Combining both limits, write u(ρ) = e−ρ/2 · ρl+1 · f(ρ), where f(ρ) is a function that must be well-behaved (finite polynomial at both ends). The entire quantisation problem reduces to finding when f(ρ) is a polynomial.

The Series Solution — Frobenius Method

▸ Full Series Solution: Quantisation of Energy

Substitute u(ρ) = e−ρ/2 f(ρ) to remove the asymptotic behaviour, then f(ρ) = ρl+1 g(ρ). The equation for f(ρ) becomes:

[d²/dρ² − d/dρ − l(l+1)/ρ² + λ/ρ] f(ρ) = 0

Substituting f = ρl+1g, the equation for g(ρ) becomes:

ρ d²g/dρ² + (2l+2−ρ) dg/dρ + (λ−l−1) g(ρ) = 0

This is the Confluent Hypergeometric (Kummer's) equation. Try a power series:

g(ρ) = Σk=0∞ ck ρk

Substituting and equating coefficients of ρk:

ck+1/ck = (k − λ + l + 1) / [(k+1)(2l+2+k)] Recurrence relation for series coefficients

For large k: ck+1/ck ~ 1/k → the series behaves like e+ρ (same as expansion of eρ). Then u ~ ρl+1e−ρ/2·e+ρ ~ ρl+1e+ρ/2 → diverges at ρ → ∞. This is not admissible.

Termination Condition: The series must terminate after a finite number of terms. If ck+1 = 0 for some k = p:

p − λ + l + 1 = 0  →  λ = p + l + 1 p = 0,1,2,... (radial quantum number)

Define the principal quantum number n = p + l + 1, so λ = n.

But λ = Ze²/(4πε₀ℏ)·(−μ/2E)1/2 must be an integer n → Energy is quantised!
En = −μZ²e⁴ / (2ℏ²(4πε₀)² n²) = −Z²·13.6 eV / n² Hydrogen energy levels emerge from series termination — quantisation!
The Principal Quantum Number n
The integer λ is conventionally denoted n — the principal quantum number.
  • n = p + l + 1, where p = 0,1,2,... is the radial quantum number
  • For fixed n: lmax = n−1 (when p = 0, all amplitude in angular motion)
  • For fixed n: l ranges from 0 to n−1
  • For fixed n and l: m ranges from −l to +l (2l+1 values)

Important Questions — Lecture 4

Why must the power series terminate? What happens if it doesn't?
If the power series g(ρ) continues infinitely, the ratio of successive terms ck+1/ck → 1/k for large k, which is the same growth rate as the Taylor series of eρ. In that case, u(ρ) ~ e−ρ/2 · e+ρ · ρl+1 = ρl+1 e+ρ/2, which diverges exponentially as ρ → ∞. A divergent u means the probability density |Ψ|² grows without bound at large r — the electron would be found with equal probability everywhere in space, which is not physical for a bound state. Therefore, the series must terminate at a finite order p, giving a polynomial. This is the quantisation condition, and it is what forces λ = n to be an integer. The energy quantisation of hydrogen is not put in by hand — it emerges from the requirement of normalisability.
Examine the behaviour of the H-atom radial function near the nucleus and far from it. Exam Q
Near nucleus (r → 0): The radial function Rnl(r) ~ rl from the Frobenius analysis. For l = 0 (s-states): R ~ r⁰ = constant → |Ψ|² is finite at the origin, meaning there is non-zero probability of finding the electron at the nucleus. For l ≥ 1: R ~ rl → 0, so the electron avoids the nucleus due to the centrifugal barrier.

Far from nucleus (r → ∞): R ~ e−r/na₀ where a₀ = Bohr radius. The probability decays exponentially at large distances. The characteristic length scale is na₀ — for higher n, the electron is found further from the nucleus.

Numerical estimate for 1s (from notes): ψ1s = 2(1/a₀)3/2 e−r/a₀, with a₀ ≈ 0.53 Å. At r = 1.5 Å (typical nuclear dimension is ~10−15 m ≈ 10−5 Å), the wavefunction is not zero but is extremely small. So ψ1s ≠ 0 at r = 0, but the probability of finding the electron inside the nucleus is negligible. Refer to B&J eq. 7.56 through 7.58.

Associated Laguerre Polynomials & Radial Wavefunctions

16 / 03 / 2026

From Series to Special Polynomials

The terminated power series g(ρ) that solves the radial equation is identified as an Associated Laguerre Polynomial. The full radial wavefunction is:

Rnl(r) = Nl e−ρ/2 ρl Ln+l2l+1(ρ)   [equivalently using ₁F₁]

Rnl(r) = NH e−ρ/2 ρl 1F1(l+1−n, 2l+2; ρ) ρ = 2Zr/na₀; N = normalisation constant; a₀ = Bohr radius

Vector Space and Completeness

Hermitian Operators & Vector Space — Why This Matters
The Hamiltonian Ĥ is a Hermitian operator: Ĥ† = Ĥ. Three key properties of Hermitian operators:
  1. All eigenvalues are real (energies are real — physically required)
  2. Eigenfunctions are orthonormal: ⟨ψn|ψm⟩ = δnm
  3. Completeness condition: the eigenfunctions form a basis set (complete set) — any function can be expanded as a linear combination of eigenfunctions
This completeness is what allows perturbation theory: if the potential is slightly distorted, the solution can be constructed using the unperturbed eigenfunctions as a basis. The solution space is a vector space (function space / Hilbert space), with eigenfunctions as basis vectors.

Structure of Radial Wavefunctions

Nodes of the Radial Distribution Function
The radial distribution function r²|Rnl|² has exactly (n−l−1) nodes (zeros, not counting r = 0 and r = ∞). This equals the radial quantum number p = n−l−1.
  • 1s (n=1, l=0): p = 0 nodes → single maximum
  • 2s (n=2, l=0): p = 1 node → two maxima
  • 2p (n=2, l=1): p = 0 nodes → single maximum (but wavefunction has different shape)
  • 3s (n=3, l=0): p = 2 nodes → three maxima

Why Does ψ1s(r=0) ≠ 0 But Electrons Are Not Found Inside the Nucleus?

Conceptual Point — Density vs Probability
Density = probability per unit volume = |Ψ|². At r = 0, ψ1s = 2(1/a₀)3/2 ≠ 0, so the probability density is non-zero at the origin.

However, probability = density × volume element = |Ψ|² · 4πr²dr. At r → 0: 4πr² → 0 faster than |Ψ|² remains finite. So the actual probability of finding the electron in a tiny shell around r = 0 is zero. The electron is not found inside the nucleus even though ψ ≠ 0 there. The relevant probability is:

P(r)dr = r²|Rnl(r)|² dr   ← this is the radial distribution function.

At r = 0: P(0) = 0 (for all states) since the r² factor vanishes. So SI (probability/m³) × volume → 0 at the nucleus. The answer exists "in nuclear dimension" as the notes state — the wavefunction is non-zero but the nuclear volume is so tiny that the integrated probability is negligible.

Important Questions — Lecture 5

Why are the Legendre polynomials Pl(cosθ) a Fourier series is not used? When do they appear naturally?
Fourier series expand functions on a flat domain with periodic boundary conditions (e.g., 0 to 2π). The θ coordinate on a sphere has boundary conditions at θ = 0 (north pole) and θ = π (south pole), and regularity requirements (the function must be finite and smooth there). The natural eigenfunctions of the angular part of the Laplacian on a sphere are the Legendre polynomials Pl(cosθ), which automatically satisfy these boundary conditions. They arise naturally because: (1) Legendre's differential equation appears when you separate the SE in spherical polars; (2) They are orthogonal on [−1, +1]: ∫_{-1}^{+1} Pl Pl' d(cosθ) = δ_{ll'} × 2/(2l+1); (3) They form a complete basis for functions of cosθ. Fourier series use eimφ for the φ direction (periodic on [0, 2π]) but Legendre polynomials for θ. Together they give the spherical harmonics Ylm(θ,φ).

Beyond Standard Hydrogen: H-like Atoms

H-like Isoelectronic Sequence
Any system with a single electron and nuclear charge Z has the same Hamiltonian as H (up to Z). Examples: He+ (Z=2), Li2+ (Z=3), C5+ (Z=6). The same calculation applies with Z substituted in. Energy: En = −Z²·13.6 eV/n².

Key question: What differs between H, D, T (isotopes)? → Only the nuclear mass → only the reduced mass μ → only the energy scale. The angular distribution (Ylm) is unchanged; only energy levels shift.

Fermi-Teller Radius
The Fermi-Teller radius is the minimum distance between two particles at which they can form a bound system. Used in gravitational cosmology. It defines the smallest stable "atom-like" configuration. For our purposes: it characterises the size below which classical orbits break down and quantum treatment is essential.

Rydberg Atoms

A Rydberg atom has one electron promoted to a very high quantum number n. Near the highest angular momentum state (l = n−1), the orbit becomes approximately circular → circular Rydberg state.

Properties of Circular Rydberg States
  • l = n−1 (maximum angular momentum for given n) → circular orbit (Bohr correspondence)
  • Very high polarisability — can detect extremely small electric/magnetic field fluctuations
  • Very long lifetime — dipole transitions to lower states are heavily suppressed for high n (selection rules + small matrix elements)
  • Detection methods: laser excitation, microwave excitation, state-selective field ionisation
  • For the highest k (Sommerfeld) value → circular orbit = Bohr atom (Sommerfeld's theorem)
Size of an Atom — What Does It Mean?
The "size" of an atom is the expectation value ⟨r⟩ in the relevant quantum state. For the highest angular momentum state (circular Rydberg state, l = n−1): the orbit is circular, and ⟨r⟩ ≈ n²a₀/Z. For very large n, ⟨r⟩ → n²a₀ — Rydberg atoms can be as large as micrometres! This is why Rydberg atoms respond to tiny fields: a small field exerts a force F = eE at large r, giving a large torque. Also, for very high n and l = n−1, the state corresponds to a Bohr orbit — the quantum and classical pictures merge (Bohr's correspondence principle).

Important Questions — Lecture 6

Write down the Hamiltonian for anti-hydrogen. How does it differ from hydrogen?
Anti-hydrogen consists of an antiproton (p̄, charge −e, mass mp) and a positron (e+, charge +e, mass me). The kinetic energy terms are identical to hydrogen (same masses). The interaction potential: the positron (+e) and antiproton (−e) attract each other, so the Coulomb term is negative (attractive), exactly like H. Therefore: Ĥanti-H = −ℏ²/2mp∇²p̄ − ℏ²/2me∇²e+ − e²/(4πε₀r₁₂). This is identical to the hydrogen Hamiltonian! Anti-hydrogen has exactly the same energy levels, spectral lines, and quantum numbers as hydrogen — this is required by CPT symmetry (charge conjugation + parity + time reversal invariance). Experimental verification of this is a major goal of the ALPHA experiment at CERN.
For positronium (e⁺e⁻), what is the reduced mass and how do the energy levels compare to hydrogen?
For positronium: m₁ = m₂ = me → μ = me²/(2me) = me/2. Since Z = 1 (one unit charge on the positron), the energy levels: En = −(μ/me)(Z²×13.6)/n² = −(1/2)(13.6)/n² = −6.8 eV/n². All energy levels are exactly half those of hydrogen, and the Bohr radius is doubled: aPs = 2a₀. The spectral lines of positronium are redshifted by a factor of 2 compared to hydrogen — this has been experimentally confirmed, providing a clean test of QED.

Two-Electron Atoms — Structure & the Three-Body Problem

06 / 04 / 2026

The Helium Atom — A Three-Body Problem

The He atom has a nucleus (charge Ze) and two electrons at (r⃗₁, θ₁, φ₁) and (r⃗₂, θ₂, φ₂). This is a three-body problem — it cannot be solved exactly in closed form.

Why This is Hard — The Coupling Problem
The H-atom had one electron in the Coulomb field of the nucleus. The problem was separable. For He, there are now three terms in the Hamiltonian, and the critical difference is the electron-electron repulsion e²/4πε₀r₁₂. This term couples the two electrons — it depends on both r⃗₁ and r⃗₂ simultaneously. Therefore [Ĥ, L₁²] ≠ 0 and [Ĥ, L₂²] ≠ 0. Neither l₁ nor l₂ is a good quantum number separately!
Ĥ = −ℏ²/2m ∇₁² − Ze²/4πε₀r₁ − ℏ²/2m ∇₂² − Ze²/4πε₀r₂ + e²/4πε₀r₁₂

= H₁ + H₂ + H₁₂ He Hamiltonian: H₁ and H₂ are H-like; H₁₂ is the coupling (electron repulsion)

Where r₁₂ = |r⃗₁ − r⃗₂| = (r₁² + r₂² − 2r₁r₂ cosθ)1/2. Note: r₁₂ can be expanded in Legendre polynomials when r₁ > r₂:

1/r₁₂ = Σl (r₂l/r₁l+1) Pl(cosθ)   (for r₁ > r₂) Legendre polynomial expansion — multipole expansion

Degrees of Freedom — Reducing the Problem

Start with 9 coordinates (3 nuclear + 3 for e₁ + 3 for e₂) — technically even nuclear coordinates, though usually the nucleus is treated as fixed in the Born-Oppenheimer approximation.
Translational symmetry → separate CM motion → 6 DoF remaining (relative coordinates of two electrons with respect to nucleus).
Unlike H-atom: [Ĥ, L₁²] ≠ 0 because H₁₂ mixes different l₁ values. So l₁, l₂ separately are not conserved.
However, the total angular momentum L⃗ = L⃗₁ + L⃗₂ does commute with Ĥ (overall rotational symmetry preserved). So total L, total S, and total J are good quantum numbers.
Pauli Exclusion Principle becomes crucial: exchange degeneracy must be handled by requiring antisymmetric total wavefunctions.

The Permutation / Exchange Operator

Many-Electron Systems and Indistinguishability
Electrons are indistinguishable. The physical state cannot depend on which label we assign to which electron. The permutation operator P̂ij swaps all coordinates (spatial + spin) of electrons i and j:

P̂ij Ψ(q₁,...,qᵢ,...,qⱼ,...,qN) = αΨ(q₁,...,qⱼ,...,qᵢ,...,qN)

Since P̂²ij = 1 → eigenvalues α = ±1. For fermions (electrons): α = −1 → antisymmetric. For bosons: α = +1 → symmetric.
Mathematics of the Pauli Exclusion Principle
For antisymmetric states (α = −1): if two electrons have identical quantum numbers qᵢ = qⱼ, then: Ψ(q₁,...,qᵢ,...,qᵢ,...) = −Ψ(q₁,...,qᵢ,...,qᵢ,...) → Ψ = 0.

A wavefunction that equals its own negative must be zero. This is the mathematical embodiment of the Pauli exclusion principle: no two electrons can have the same complete set of quantum numbers (n, l, m, ms).

The Spin-Orbital Wavefunction

The total two-electron wavefunction must be antisymmetric. It factors as spatial × spin:

Ψn(q) = Ψ(r⃗₁, r⃗₂) · χ(1,2) q = all coordinates (spatial + spin). The total function must be antisymmetric.

Since Pauli requires the total to be antisymmetric:

  • If spatial part is symmetric → spin part must be antisymmetric
  • If spatial part is antisymmetric → spin part must be symmetric

Important Questions — Lecture 7

Why do l₁ and l₂ fail to be good quantum numbers in helium?
A quantum number is "good" if the corresponding operator commutes with the Hamiltonian. In H-like atoms, each electron's Hamiltonian H₁ = −ℏ²/2m∇₁² − Ze²/4πε₀r₁ commutes with L₁² because the potential depends only on r₁. In helium, the full Hamiltonian Ĥ = H₁ + H₂ + H₁₂. The term H₁₂ = e²/4πε₀r₁₂ depends on both r⃗₁ and r⃗₂ — it has no reason to commute with L₁² alone: [H₁₂, L₁²] ≠ 0. Therefore [Ĥ, L₁²] ≠ 0, so l₁ is not conserved. The electron-electron repulsion continuously transfers angular momentum between the two electrons. However, [Ĥ, L²total] = 0 since H₁₂ depends only on r₁₂ which is invariant under overall rotations → total L is conserved.

General Rules for Adding Angular Momenta

Given two angular momenta j⃗₁ (quantum number j₁) and j⃗₂ (quantum number j₂), their sum j⃗ = j⃗₁ + j⃗₂ has:

Addition of Angular Momentum — Key Rules
Allowed values of j: j = |j₁−j₂|, |j₁−j₂|+1, ..., j₁+j₂ (in integer steps)

For each j: mj ranges from −j to +j in integer steps (2j+1 values)

Uncoupled basis: |j₁, mj₁, j₂, mj₂⟩ (labelled by individual z-projections)

Coupled basis: |j, mj, j₁, j₂⟩ (labelled by total j)

The transformation between the two bases involves the Clebsch-Gordan coefficients.

Important Questions — Lecture 8

What are Clebsch-Gordan coefficients and where do they appear?
Clebsch-Gordan (CG) coefficients are the transformation coefficients between the uncoupled basis {|j₁,m₁,j₂,m₂⟩} and the coupled basis {|j,m_j,j₁,j₂⟩}: |j, m_j⟩ = Σm₁,m₂ ⟨j₁,m₁,j₂,m₂|j,m_j⟩ |j₁,m₁⟩|j₂,m₂⟩. The quantities ⟨j₁,m₁,j₂,m₂|j,m_j⟩ are the CG coefficients. They are tabulated (Condon and Shortley convention). They appear in: (1) atomic spectroscopy — coupling L and S to get J; (2) nuclear physics — coupling nucleon angular momenta; (3) Wigner-Eckart theorem — simplifying matrix elements in perturbation theory; (4) quantum computing — entangled state construction. They are non-zero only when m_j = m₁ + m₂ (z-component addition) and |j₁−j₂| ≤ j ≤ j₁+j₂ (triangle rule).
Explain exchange degeneracy and why it matters for He energy levels.
For two identical particles, if we swap their labels (1↔2), the physical state is unchanged. But for a non-symmetric Hamiltonian, the states Ψ(r⃗₁,r⃗₂) and Ψ(r⃗₂,r⃗₁) would have the same energy — this is exchange degeneracy. The true eigenstates are not Ψ(r⃗₁,r⃗₂) alone but symmetric/antisymmetric combinations: Ψ± = [Ψ(r⃗₁,r⃗₂) ± Ψ(r⃗₂,r⃗₁)]/√2. The Pauli principle resolves this degeneracy by requiring the total wavefunction to be antisymmetric. This splitting is energetically significant: the symmetric (ortho-He) and antisymmetric (para-He) spatial states have different energies because the electron-electron repulsion ⟨e²/4πε₀r₁₂⟩ has different values when electrons avoid each other (antisymmetric) vs. can be close (symmetric). This is the origin of Hund's rule: triplet (antisymmetric spatial) states lie lower in energy because electrons avoid each other → less repulsion.

Why Ortho is Lower than Para (Same Config)

Hund's First Rule — Physical Origin
For the same electron configuration (e.g., 1s2s), the triplet (S=1, ortho) lies lower in energy than the singlet (S=0, para). Why?

The triplet has an antisymmetric spatial wavefunction → electrons are more likely to be far apart (less probable to be at the same point) → lower electron-electron repulsion energy → lower total energy.

The singlet has a symmetric spatial wavefunction → electrons can be close → higher repulsion → higher energy.

This is the physical basis of Hund's first rule: for equivalent electrons, the term with highest multiplicity (highest S) lies lowest in energy.

Inter-system Transitions and Forbidden Lines

ΔS = 0 Selection Rule and Intercombination Lines
In LS coupling, the selection rule ΔS = 0 forbids transitions between singlet and triplet states. Therefore ortho-He and para-He have separate spectra and do not interconvert by radiation. This caused early spectroscopists to think helium was two different elements ("parhelium" and "orthohelium")!

When spin-orbit coupling is strong (high-Z elements), ΔS = 0 breaks down → intercombination lines appear. For example, the 254 nm Hg line ³P₁ → ¹S₀ is an intercombination line of great practical importance (UV lamps, atomic clocks).

Why does helium have two distinct spectroscopic series (ortho-He and para-He)?
Helium has two electrons. Their spins can combine to S=0 (singlet, para-He) or S=1 (triplet, ortho-He). In LS coupling, the electric dipole selection rule ΔS = 0 means a photon cannot flip the total electron spin. Therefore, transitions between singlet and triplet states are forbidden. The para-He and ortho-He states are energetically distinct (exchange splitting) and do not communicate spectroscopically. This gives two completely independent sets of spectral lines — as if they were two different atoms. Historically, the "two heliums" were called parahelium and orthohelium and were thought to be separate elements. The puzzle was resolved when Heisenberg explained in 1926 that exchange degeneracy and the Pauli principle cause the singlet-triplet splitting and the selection rule ΔS = 0 forbids intercombination.
What is the lowest state of the He atom? Give its quantum numbers and term symbol.
Ground state: 1s² configuration. Both electrons in the n=1, l=0 orbital. Since both spatial quantum numbers are identical, Pauli Exclusion forces the spin part to be antisymmetric → singlet state S=0. Total: S=0 → 2S+1=1, L=0 → l₁+l₂=0, J=|L−S|=0. Term symbol: ¹S₀. This is the para-He ground state. The energy in atomic units: E = 2×(−Z²/2n²) + ⟨1/r₁₂⟩ ≈ −4 + 5/8 = −2.75 a.u. ≈ −74.8 eV (the exact experimental value is −79.0 eV; the discrepancy is due to electron correlation). The simple orbital model gives a first approximation; perturbation theory and variational methods improve it.
