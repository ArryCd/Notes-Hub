Introduction to Solid State Physics

05 / 02 / 2026

What is Solid State Physics?

Solid State Physics studies matter in its densest form — the crystalline solid — where atoms are packed close enough that their mutual interactions fundamentally alter every property. It spans electrical, magnetic, thermal, optical, and mechanical behaviour of solids.

Why is the density so high?
At T = 300 K, thermal energy kBT ≈ 26 meV. Bonding energies in solids are typically 1–10 eV — orders of magnitude larger. So at room temperature, the crystal lattice is essentially rigid; atoms just vibrate around fixed sites.

Chemical Bonding in Solids

The nature of bonding determines almost everything about a solid's properties:

CovalentDiamondShared electron pairs; directional~3–8 eV/bond
MetallicCu, FeSea of delocalised electrons binding positive ions~1–5 eV/atom
Van der WaalsSolid Ar, organic crystalsFluctuating dipole–dipole (London dispersion)~10–100 meV
HydrogenIceH atom shared between electronegative atoms~0.1–0.5 eV

Energy Scales and Why they Matter

keV – MeV (Intermediate)Nuclear structure; nucleus is no longer a point — nucleons matterX-rays (keV), γ-rays
MeV – GeV (High Energy)Quark–gluon physicsParticle accelerators
Optical probe in SSP
Optical / EM radiation (UV-Visible-IR) carries photon energies from meV to a few keV — perfectly matched to electronic transitions in solids. X-rays (λ ~ 1 Å, hν ~ keV) are used to determine crystal structure via diffraction.

Conductivity — the Central Observable

Conductivity σ spans an enormous range:

σ(metal) ~ 106 Ω−1cm−1   ↔   σ(insulator) ~ 10−20 Ω−1cm−1

That is 26 orders of magnitude! SSP explains this using band structure — the way quantum mechanics determines which electron energies are allowed or forbidden in a periodic potential.

Band Structure: A First Glimpse

In a crystal, the energy levels of ~1023 electrons per cm3 bunch into quasi-continuous bands separated by forbidden gaps. The energy separation between adjacent allowed levels within a band is ~10−18 eV — effectively continuous.

The key physical distinction
Metal: outermost band partially filled → electrons at the Fermi level can be excited by any tiny electric field → conduction.
Insulator: outermost occupied band completely filled, gap ≫ kBT → no available states nearby → no conduction.
Semiconductor: gap small (~1 eV) → thermal excitation at room temperature can bridge it.

Free Electron Gas: The Drude Model

05 / 02 / 2026 & 12 / 02 / 2026 — Ref: Ashcroft & Mermin

The Free Electron Gas Model

Proposed by Drude (1900), this classical model treats the conduction electrons of a metal as an ideal gas of free particles, applying the Kinetic Theory of Gases (KTG). The positive ion cores are ignored except as scattering centres.

Assumptions of the Drude Model
1. Electrons are free and non-interacting between collisions.
2. Collisions are instantaneous and random, characterised by a mean free time (relaxation time) τ.
3. After a collision, an electron's velocity is random (no memory of previous velocity).
4. The classical Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution governs electron speeds.

Crystal Structure: Packing Fractions

Before computing conductivity, we need the electron density n. This requires knowing how many atoms fit in a unit cell.

StructureAtoms/cellPacking FractionExamples
Simple Cubic (SC)1π/6 ≈ 52%Po
Body-Centred Cubic (BCC)2π√3/8 ≈ 68%Na, K, W, Fe
Face-Centred Cubic (FCC)4π√2/6 ≈ 74%Cu, Al, Ni, Au

For copper (Cu): atomic weight 63.546 g/mol, density 8.96 g/cm³. Number density: n = (6.022×1023 / 63.546) × 8.96 ≈ 8.5 × 1022 atoms/cm3. Each Cu atom contributes 1 conduction electron → ne ≈ 8.5 × 1022 e⁻/cm3.

Drude Equation of Motion

An electron in an electric field E experiences force F = eE (e > 0 is the elementary charge magnitude; the electron has charge −e). Including a resistive drag term proportional to velocity:

m dv/dt = eE − (m/τ)v

The resistive term models the effect of collisions: on average, an electron that has been drifting for time τ will collide and lose its directed velocity. The factor m/τ is called the damping coefficient.

▸ Full Derivation: Drift Velocity and Conductivity

At steady state, dv/dt = 0:

eE = (m/τ)vd  →  vd = eEτ/m

Current density j = nevd (n = electron density, e = charge magnitude):

j = ne · (eEτ/m) = (ne²τ/m)E

Comparing with Ohm's law j = σE:

σ = ne²τ/mDrude Conductivity Formula

For the transient case, solving m dv/dt = eE − mv/τ with v(0) = 0:

v(t) = vd(1 − e−t/τ)

At t → ∞, v → vd. The characteristic time to reach steady state is τ ~ 10−14 s.

Numerical Success of Drude

Using n ~ 1022/cc, e = 1.6×10−19 C, m = 9.1×10−31 kg, τ ~ 10−14 s:

σ = (1022×106 × (1.6×10−19)² × 10−14) / (9.1×10−31) ~ 106 Ω−1cm−1

This matches experiment! The Drude model successfully predicts room-temperature conductivity of metals.

Hall Effect and Drude

The Hall effect provides a crucial test: apply E in ŷ direction and B in ẑ direction. The Lorentz force pushes electrons in x̂. At steady state, a transverse Hall field Ex builds up.

RH = −1/(ne)     (Hall coefficient from Drude)

For monovalent metals (Cu, Ag), RH is measured to be ~ −1.5/(ne), in good agreement. However, for divalent/trivalent metals (Be, Al), RH comes out positive experimentally — meaning the carriers behave as positive holes. Drude cannot explain this; it requires quantum band theory.

Thermal Conductivity and Wiedemann–Franz Law

The Drude model also successfully derives thermal conductivity κ. In a temperature gradient, electrons near the hot end carry more energy than those near the cold end:

κ = (nv²τ/3) · (dE/dT)  =  (1/3)n⟨v²⟩τ · Cve

The ratio κ/σT = (4/π)(kB/e)² = constant — this is the Wiedemann–Franz Law, a major success of the Drude model.

Failures of the Drude Model

Major Failures
1. Specific heat of electrons: Drude predicts Cve = (3/2)NkB. Experiment gives a value ~100× smaller at room temperature. Drude counts all electrons as contributing, but only those near EF do.

2. Low-temperature conductivity: At low T, τ → 10−13 s, giving mean free path λ ~ 10−7 m = 1000 Å. But the lattice spacing is ~1 Å — electrons should scatter much more. This is unexplained.

3. Positive Hall coefficients: Cannot explain hole-like carriers in divalent metals.

4. Insulators: Drude assumes all valence electrons are free, predicting similar conductivity for all materials. Cannot explain insulators.

The resolution of all these failures requires quantum mechanics — specifically, Fermi–Dirac statistics and the concept of energy bands.

Sommerfeld / Quantum Free Electron Theory

19 / 02 / 2026 — Ref: Kittel / Dekker

Why Quantum Mechanics?

Electrons are quantum particles — they obey Fermi–Dirac statistics, not Maxwell–Boltzmann. This one change resolves almost all failures of the Drude model.

Key Conceptual Difference: Standing vs. Progressive Waves
The Drude model confines electrons to a box with hard-wall boundary conditions → standing waves (sin functions).

The Sommerfeld model uses periodic boundary conditions (Born–von Kármán conditions) → progressive / travelling waves eikx. This is physically appropriate because electrons in a solid circulate through the crystal, not bounce off walls. In practice, both give the same density of states, but the physics picture is cleaner with progressive waves.

1D Quantum Well: Standing Wave Approach

Consider an electron in a 1D infinite well [0, L]. The Schrödinger equation:

−(ℏ²/2m)d²ψ/dx² = Eψ   →   ψ(x) = √(2/L) sin(nπx/L)
En = ℏ²k²/2m = n²π²ℏ²/(2mL²)     k = nπ/L

Energy goes as n². In 3D, for a cube of side L: Enx,ny,nz = (π²ℏ²/2mL²)(nx² + ny² + nz²).

Progressive Wave (Periodic BC) Approach

Require ψ(x+L) = ψ(x). Then k = 2πn/L, and the wavefunction is a plane wave:

ψ(x) = (1/√V)eikx   →   in 3D: ψ(r) = (1/√V)eik·r

Each k-state is doubly degenerate (spin up and down). States form a k-space lattice with unit cell volume (2π/L)³.

Fermi–Dirac Distribution

Electrons, being fermions, obey the Pauli exclusion principle — no two electrons can share the same quantum state. The probability of a state with energy E being occupied at temperature T is:

fD(E) = 1/(1 + e(E−EF)/kBT)Fermi–Dirac Distribution

At T = 0: f = 1 for E < EF, f = 0 for E > EF. The Fermi energy EF is the highest occupied energy level at T = 0.

Physical meaning of EF
For typical metals, EF ~ 3–7 eV. Room temperature kBT ~ 0.026 eV. Since kBT ≪ EF, only electrons within ~kBT of the Fermi surface can be thermally excited. This is why only ~1% of electrons participate in thermal processes — explaining the 100× discrepancy in specific heat!

Density of States in 3D

The density of states D(E) tells us how many electron states exist per unit energy interval:

D(E) = (V/2π²)(2m/ℏ²)3/2 E1/2 = C·E1/2

This E1/2 dependence (parabolic DOS) is a key feature of the free electron model in 3D. The number of electrons in dE is: N(E)dE = D(E)f(E)dE.

Fermi Energy at T = 0

Fill all states up to EF₀ (ground state Fermi energy):

N = ∫₀EF₀ D(E)dE = (2/3)CEF₀3/2
EF₀ = (ℏ²/2m)(3π²n)2/3Ground-state Fermi Energy

For Cu: n = 8.5×1022/cm³ → EF₀ ≈ 7 eV. Corresponds to TF = EF/kB ≈ 80,000 K! This "quantum temperature" is far above room temperature, confirming that the electron gas is always highly degenerate.

Average Energy at T = 0

Ē = (3/5)EF₀

Compare with classical result (3/2)kBT — for copper at room T, the quantum average is ~100× larger. Quantum zero-point motion (Pauli pressure) is enormous.

Temperature Dependence of the Fermi Gas

26 / 02 / 2026 – 19 / 03 / 2026

Temperature Dependence of EF

At finite T, some electrons near EF₀ get thermally excited to higher states. The Fermi level shifts slightly:

EF(T) = EF₀[1 − (π²/12)(kBT/EF₀)²]

The correction is of order (kBT/EF₀)² ~ 10−4 at room temperature — EF barely changes. This is why tables list a single Fermi energy for metals.

Average Energy at Finite T

Ū(T) = U₀[1 + (5π²/12)(kBT/EF)²]

Electronic Heat Capacity — Sommerfeld's Triumph

Differentiating with respect to T:

Cve/vol = (π²/2)nkB(kBT/EF) = γT

where γ = π²nkB²/(2EF) is the Sommerfeld parameter. This is linear in T — far smaller than the classical (3/2)NkB. Physically, only electrons within kBT of EF can be excited.

Experimental test: Cv/T vs T²
At low T, total specific heat Cv/vol = γT + AT³, where the AT³ term is the Debye lattice contribution. Plotting Cv/T vs T² gives a straight line with intercept γ (electronic) and slope A (lattice). This is a standard experimental method to extract EF from measurements.

Sommerfeld Conductivity

In Sommerfeld theory, only electrons near EF contribute to transport. The conductivity formula is the same form as Drude but τ is now evaluated at the Fermi energy:

σ = ne²τ(EF)/m

Mean free path: λ = vFτ(EF), where vF = √(2EF/m) ~ 106 m/s is the Fermi velocity. At room T, λ ~ 100–1000 Å — much larger than the lattice spacing, because quantum mechanics forbids scattering from a perfectly periodic lattice (Bloch states are exact eigenstates).

Why is λ ≫ a at low T?

Bloch's Theorem — No Scattering from Perfect Lattice
This is a profound quantum result: an electron in a perfectly periodic potential propagates without scattering — it is an eigenstate (Bloch state) of the Hamiltonian. Scattering only occurs from deviations from periodicity: thermal vibrations (phonons), impurities, defects. At low T, phonons freeze out → τ → ∞, λ → ∞ → resistivity → 0.

Pauli Spin Paramagnetism

Free electrons have spin magnetic moment μB = eℏ/2mc. In a magnetic field B, spin-up and spin-down states split by 2μBB. At T=0, more spins align with the field, creating a net magnetisation:

χP = nμB²/kBTF = (3nμB²)/(2EF)

This is temperature-independent — unlike classical paramagnetism which goes as 1/T (Curie law). The experimental observation of T-independent paramagnetism in alkali metals was early evidence for Fermi–Dirac statistics.

Band Theory: Periodic Potential & Bloch's Theorem

02 / 04 / 2026

Beyond the Free Electron Model

The free electron model ignores the periodic array of positive ion cores. In reality, the electron moves in a potential V(x) that has the periodicity of the lattice:

V(x) = V(x + a)     (a = lattice constant)

The Schrödinger equation is then:

−(ℏ²/2m)d²ψ/dx² + V(x)ψ(x) = Eψ(x)

For a free electron (V = 0): solutions are plane waves ψ = Ae±ikx. But with periodic V, the plain wave is modulated.

Bloch's Theorem

Bloch's Theorem (1928)
The eigenfunction of a Hamiltonian with a periodic potential can always be written as:
ψk(x) = eikxuk(x)     where     uk(x) = uk(x+a)
That is, the wavefunction is a plane wave eikx modulated by a function uk(x) that has the same periodicity as the lattice. This is Bloch's theorem, and the solutions are called Bloch states or Bloch waves.

This is non-trivial — it tells us that despite the complicated periodic potential, the electron still propagates like a plane wave (it's just not a simple plane wave). The crystal momentum ℏk is a good quantum number.

Consequences of Bloch's Theorem

Why no scattering from a perfect lattice?
Because Bloch states are exact eigenstates of the periodic Hamiltonian. The electron "knows about" all the ion cores simultaneously through the periodic potential — it propagates without being scattered. This is the quantum mechanical explanation for why metals are good conductors: only deviations from perfect periodicity (phonons, impurities) cause resistance.

The Kronig–Penney Model

02 / 04 / 2026

The Model Potential

The Kronig–Penney (KP) model is the simplest tractable model of a periodic potential. Instead of the smooth Coulomb potential of actual ion cores, we use a periodic square-wave potential:

V(x) = 0   for   0 < x < a     (well region)
V(x) = V₀   for   −b < x < 0     (barrier region)

with period (a + b). The barrier represents the potential energy hill near the ion cores. We assume V₀ > E (the electron energy is below the barrier top, so classically it would be forbidden in the barrier region, but quantum tunnelling allows it through).

Setting Up the Equations

In region 0 < x < a (well): −(ℏ²/2m)d²ψ/dx² = Eψ → solution ψ₁ with parameter α² = 2mE/ℏ²
In region −b < x < 0 (barrier, V₀ > E): d²ψ/dx² = β²ψ → solution ψ₂ with β² = 2m(V₀−E)/ℏ²

▸ Full Derivation: Bloch Condition → KP Dispersion Relation

Using Bloch's theorem ψ = eikxuk(x), substitute into the Schrödinger equation to get equations for u1 and u2:

d²u₁/dx² + 2ik(du₁/dx) + (α²−k²)u₁ = 0   — (3)
d²u₂/dx² + 2ik(du₂/dx) − (β²+k²)u₂ = 0   — (4)

Try u = Aemx. From (3): m² + 2ikm + (α²−k²) = 0 → m = −ik ± iα → m = i(α−k) or −i(α+k)

u₁ = Aei(α−k)x + Be−i(α+k)x   — (5)

From (4): m² + 2ikm − (β²+k²) = 0 → m = −ik ± β → m = (β−ik) or −(β+ik)

u₂ = Ce(β−ik)x + De−(β+ik)x   — (6)

Apply 4 boundary conditions (ψ and dψ/dx continuous at x=0 and x=a, using periodicity at x=−b):

A + B = C + D  — (7)
i(α−k)A − i(α+k)B = (β−ik)C − (β+ik)D  — (8)
Aei(α−k)a + Be−i(α+k)a = Ce−(β−ik)b + De(β+ik)b  — (9)
...  — (10)

For non-trivial solutions, the 4×4 determinant must vanish. After algebra:

[(β²−α²)/(2αβ)]sinh(βb)sin(αa) + cosh(βb)cos(αa) = cos[k(a+b)]General KP Dispersion Relation — Eq. (11)

This is the central result. The left side is a function of energy E (through α and β), the right side is bounded by ±1. Solutions only exist where |LHS| ≤ 1 → this gives allowed energy bands.

The Dirac-Comb Limit: P Model

Take V₀ → ∞ and b → 0 while keeping P = mV₀ab/ℏ² finite (a "Dirac comb" of delta function barriers). Then β ≫ α, sinh(βb) ≈ βb, cosh(βb) ≈ 1, β²≫α²:

(P/αa)sin(αa) + cos(αa) = cos(ka)KP Model (Dirac comb form)

where P = mV₀ab/ℏ² is a dimensionless strength parameter representing the height of the potential barriers. The LHS is plotted as a function of αa (∝ √E).

Physical Interpretation: Three Limiting Cases

Case 1: P very large (strong barriers)

f(E) oscillates wildly → very narrow allowed bands. In the extreme P → ∞: the bands shrink to discrete lines at αa = nπ → α = nπ/a → E = n²π²ℏ²/(2ma²). These are the energy levels of an infinite square well — electrons are completely trapped and cannot move from one well to the next. This is the atomic limit.

Case 2: P → 0 (no barriers)

f(E) → cos(αa) = cos(ka) → α = k → E = ℏ²k²/(2m). This is the free electron result — continuous parabola, no gaps. The energy is continuous in k.

Case 3: General P — Energy Gaps

Gaps open at k = nπ/a (i.e., Brillouin zone boundaries). Physically, at these k values the electron wavelength exactly satisfies the Bragg condition 2a = nλ — the electron wave is strongly Bragg-reflected by the periodic lattice, leading to standing waves and a splitting of energy levels (analogous to avoided crossings in perturbation theory).

Why do gaps open at zone boundaries?
At k = π/a, there are two standing wave combinations: ψ₊ ∝ cos(πx/a) (charge density peaks at ion sites, lower energy) and ψ₋ ∝ sin(πx/a) (charge density peaks between ions, higher energy). The energy difference between these two is the band gap Eg. In the nearly-free electron model, Eg = 2|VG| where VG is the Fourier component of the potential at reciprocal lattice vector G = 2π/a.

Number of States in a Band

For a 1D crystal of N unit cells with periodic BC, the allowed k values in the first Brillouin zone are k = 2πn/L (n = 0, ±1, ..., ±N/2), giving exactly N distinct k-values. Including spin: each band holds exactly 2N electron states (where N = number of primitive unit cells).

kmax = π/a = Nπ/L  →  N distinct k values per band  →  2N states per band

Effective Mass, Velocity & the Concept of Holes

09 / 04 / 2026

Electron Velocity in a Band

An electron in a Bloch state is a wavepacket. Its group velocity (the velocity at which the wavepacket — and hence the probability density — propagates) is:

v = (1/ℏ)(dE/dk)     or     v = (1/ℏ)∇kE(k)

For a free electron E = ℏ²k²/2m → v = ℏk/m = p/m. Correct! For a Bloch electron in a band, the same formula holds but with the actual band dispersion E(k).

Effective Mass

Apply an external force F = eE (electric field). By Newton's second law, F = dp/dt = ℏ(dk/dt). The acceleration is:

a = dv/dt = (1/ℏ)(d²E/dk²)(dk/dt) = (1/ℏ²)(d²E/dk²)F

Comparing with F = m*a:

1/m* = (1/ℏ²)(d²E/dk²)   →   m* = ℏ²/(d²E/dk²)Effective Mass of an Electron

The effective mass m* captures the effect of the crystal potential. It tells us: in an applied field, the electron behaves as a free particle with mass m* — the complicated band structure is absorbed into this single parameter.

Physical Meaning of m*
● m* = m (free electron) when E = ℏ²k²/2m (parabolic band).
● m* > m when the band flattens (d²E/dk² < 1/m free value) — electron is "heavier".
● m* < m near band bottom — electron accelerates faster than free.
● m* < 0 in the upper half of a band (d²E/dk² < 0) — this leads to holes!

Holes — Empty States Acting as Positive Carriers

In the upper part of a band, m* < 0: when an electric field E is applied, these electrons decelerate — they move opposite to what you'd expect. It is more useful to think of the absence of an electron (a "hole") as a particle with:

PropertyElectron (near band top)Hole
Charge−e+e (positive!)
Effective massm* < 0mh* = −m* > 0
Wave vectorkkh = −k
Velocityvevh = ve

Holes are not real particles — they are a mathematical convenience for describing the collective behaviour of many electrons near the top of a nearly-full band. But they behave exactly like positive charge carriers and explain the positive Hall coefficients seen in Be, Al, and semiconductors.

Effective Number of Free Electrons

The "effective" number of free electrons contributing to conductivity is:

Neff = (2mL/πℏ²) |dE/dk|k₁

When the band is filled to kmax = π/a: Neff = 0 — no conductivity (insulator). When filled to k₀ where d²E/dk² is maximum: Neff = max — best conductivity.

Metal vs Insulator: Band Filling

The fundamental distinction
Metal: Outermost band half-filled (Na: 3s¹, one electron in 3s band → half full) or bands overlap.

Insulator: All bands completely filled (diamond: 4 valence electrons fill 4N states = 2 bands of 2N each; gap ~5.5 eV). No available states near EF → no conduction at T=0.

Semiconductor: Like insulator but gap small (Si: ~1.1 eV, Ge: ~0.67 eV) → thermal excitation at T=300K promotes some electrons to conduction band, leaving holes in valence band.

Metals, Insulators & Semiconductors — Band Theory Perspective

09 / 04 / 2026

Sommerfeld vs Band Theory

In Sommerfeld theory, electrons move freely — no periodic potential. In Band theory, the periodic lattice creates gaps. The Sommerfeld theory is recovered in the limit P → 0 (no barriers) from the KP model.

Electron Configuration Examples

ElementConfigBand fillingBehaviour
Na3s¹3s band: half full (1 e/N states of 2N)Metal
Be2s²2s band full, BUT overlaps with 2p bandMetal (band overlap)
Diamond C2s²2p²sp³ hybridisation → valence band full, gap 5.5 eVInsulator
Si3s²3p²Same as diamond, gap 1.1 eVSemiconductor
Ge4s²4p²Gap 0.67 eVSemiconductor

ITO and Complex Conductors

Indium Tin Oxide (ITO = Sn-coated In₂O₃) is an unusual material that is simultaneously a good electrical conductor AND optically transparent. It is widely used in touchscreens, solar cells, and LCD displays. This is only possible because its band gap falls in the UV — visible light is not absorbed — while its conduction band has enough electrons from Sn doping for conductivity.

Boltzmann Transport Equation (Sommerfeld Theory)

The full quantum treatment of conductivity uses the Boltzmann transport equation, which describes how the Fermi distribution function f(r,p,t) evolves under an applied field and collisions:

∂f/∂t|field = −v·∇rf + e[E + v×B]·∇pf

At steady state, this balances with the collision term. Using the relaxation time approximation (τ depends only on energy, not on k direction):

∂f/∂t|collision = −(f − f₀)/τ

Solving gives the Sommerfeld result σ = ne²τ(EF)/m, with the key insight that τ must be evaluated at the Fermi energy (only Fermi surface electrons contribute to transport).

σ = e²λm²/(12π³ℏ³) · SFConductivity in terms of Fermi surface area SF

This elegant result says: conductivity is proportional to the Fermi surface area. Large Fermi surface → good conductor. Insulators and semiconductors have zero Fermi surface area (band gap) → zero conductivity at T = 0.

Magnetism: Fundamentals & Classification

16 / 04 / 2026

Magnetic Field Quantities

An electric current creates a magnetic field. For a solenoid of N turns, length L, current i:

H = iN/L     (units: A/m or Oersted in CGS)

H is the magnetic field strength or magnetising field. In vacuum, the magnetic flux density is B₀ = μ₀H.

Effect of Inserting a Material

When a material is placed in the field, it develops a magnetisation M = magnetic moment per unit volume. The total B inside the material:

B = μ₀H + μ₀M = μ₀(H + M) = μH

M depends on H through the magnetic susceptibility χ: M = χH. Therefore:

B = μ₀(1 + χ)H = μrμ₀H   →   μr = 1 + χ = relative permeability

Classification of Magnetic Materials

Origin of Permanent Magnetic Moments

Sources of magnetic moment in an atom:

Orbital motion: electron revolving around nucleus = current loop → μL = −(e/2mc)L = −μB√[l(l+1)]ℏ where μB = eℏ/2mc is the Bohr magneton = 9.27 × 10⁻²⁴ J/T.
Spin angular momentum: intrinsic quantum spin → μS = −gs(e/2mc)S = ∓μB for ms = ±½. The spin g-factor gs ≈ 2.0023.
Nuclear spin: much smaller by me/mp ~ 1/1836; relevant in NMR, not bulk magnetism.

Total Angular Momentum and Landé g-factor

For an atom with total orbital angular momentum L, spin S, and total J = L + S:

μJ = −gJ(e/2mc)√[J(J+1)]ℏ
gJ = 1 + [J(J+1) + S(S+1) − L(L+1)] / [2J(J+1)]     (Landé g-factor)

In an applied field, Jz = mJℏ with mJ = −J, ..., +J (space quantisation). The (2J+1) orientations have different energies E = −μJ·H = gJμBmJH.

Classical Theory of Diamagnetism (Langevin)

Lenz's law in electricity: changing B induces an EMF opposing the change. Similarly, an orbiting electron will adjust its orbital frequency when B changes — increasing or decreasing it depending on the orbital orientation. This always produces a moment opposing B → diamagnetism.

χdia = −NZ(e²/6mc²)⟨r²⟩

where N = nucleon density, Z = atomic number, ⟨r²⟩ = mean squared orbital radius. χdia ~ −10⁻⁶ — small, negative, temperature-independent. Note: the Larmor (precession) frequency ωL = eH/2mc ~ 1011 s⁻¹.

Classical Theory of Paramagnetism (Langevin)

Each atom has a permanent dipole μ. In the absence of B, moments are randomly oriented → M = 0. In field H, the Boltzmann factor e−PE/kBT gives more weight to aligned configurations. The magnetisation:

M = nμ·L(a)     where     a = μH/kBT

L(a) = coth(a) − 1/a is the Langevin function. At ordinary temperatures (a ≪ 1):

χpara = M/H = nμ²/(3kBT) = C/T     (Curie Law)

This is the classical Curie Law. But it doesn't match experiment for transition metal ions — quantum theory is needed.

Quantum Theory of Paramagnetism

26 / 04 / 2026

Hund's Rules — Ground State of Free Atoms

To find J, L, S for any atom/ion, apply Hund's rules in order:

Hund's Three Rules
Rule 1: Maximise S (total spin) consistent with Pauli exclusion. (Parallel spins avoid each other due to antisymmetric spatial wavefunction → lower repulsion energy.)

Rule 2: Maximise L (total orbital angular momentum) consistent with rule 1. (Higher L → electrons orbit same way → avoid each other → lower energy.)

Rule 3: J = |L−S| if shell less than half-filled; J = L+S if more than half-filled. (From spin-orbit coupling: ζ(L·S), ζ > 0 for less than half-filled → aligned L and S costs energy; ζ < 0 for more than half-filled.)

Example: Fe²⁺ (3d⁶)

3d shell: l=2, ml = 2, 1, 0, −1, −2. Fill 6 electrons:

↑ ↑ ↑ ↑ ↑ ↓   (ml = 2,1,0,−1,−2)

Σms = S = 2, Σml = L = 2, J = L+S = 4 (more than half-filled).
Spectroscopic term: ⁵D₄. g-factor: gJ = 1 + [J(J+1)+S(S+1)−L(L+1)]/[2J(J+1)] = 3/2.

Example: Cr²⁺ (3d⁴)

↑ ↑ ↑ ↑   S = 2, L = 2, less than half-filled → J = |L−S| = 0 → diamagnetic! despite being a transition metal.

Quantum Brillouin Function

With space quantisation, mJ takes (2J+1) discrete values. The partition function is a finite sum (not an integral as in the classical theory). The result is the Brillouin function:

M = ng J μB BJ(a)     where     a = gJμBH/kBT
BJ(a) = [(2J+1)/2J]coth[(2J+1)a/2J] − (1/2J)coth(a/2J)Brillouin Function

Limits: For J → ∞, BJ(a) → L(a) (Langevin). For J = 1/2, B1/2(a) = tanh(a). At small a (ordinary T):

χ = M/H = ng²J(J+1)μB² / 3kBT = nμB²Peff²/3kBT

where Peff = g√[J(J+1)] is the effective Bohr magneton number.

Crystal Field (Orbital) Quenching

Why iron group ions don't follow gJ√[J(J+1)]
Transition metal ions (3d, 4d, 5d) in crystals experience a strong crystal electric field from neighbouring ligands. This field acts on the orbital motion but not on the spin. If the crystal field energy ≫ spin-orbit energy, the orbital angular momentum is "quenched" — it gets locked to the lattice and cannot precess freely around B. The orbital contribution to the magnetic moment is destroyed, leaving only spin.

Result: for iron group ions, the effective moment is ≈ gs√[S(S+1)] μB (with gs ≈ 2, L effectively = 0). This matches experiment.

Rare earth ions (4f): the 4f shell is shielded by outer 5s²5p⁶ shell → crystal field is weak → L-S coupling works → Peff = g√[J(J+1)] matches experiment beautifully.

Ferromagnetism & the Weiss Molecular Field Theory

03 / 05 / 2026 (Online)

What is Ferromagnetism?

Ferromagnets exhibit spontaneous magnetisation — they remain magnetised even after the external field is removed. Below the Curie temperature TC, ferromagnets are permanently magnetised; above TC, thermal fluctuations destroy the order and the material becomes paramagnetic.

Gd2922010

Weiss Molecular Field Theory

Weiss (1907) postulated that there exists an internal "molecular field" Heff proportional to the magnetisation:

Heff = Hext + λM     (λ = Weiss constant)

This field aligns the moments cooperatively. The magnetisation is then found self-consistently using the Brillouin function:

M = MsBJ(a)     where     a = gJμBHeff/kBT

These two equations must be solved simultaneously for M — this is done graphically by finding the intersection of the Brillouin curve with a straight line.

Finding TC

For Hext = 0, spontaneous solutions for M exist when the slope of M(a) at a=0 exceeds the slope of the straight line (equation 1). This gives:

TC = nλg²J(J+1)μB² / (3kB) = nλμ² / (3kB)

where μ = gμB√[J(J+1)] is the total magnetic moment per atom.

θf ∝ λ     (Curie temperature proportional to Weiss constant)

Curie–Weiss Law (Above TC)

For T > TC, the system is paramagnetic with an applied field H. Expanding for small a:

χ = M/H = C/(T − θf)     (Curie–Weiss Law)χ diverges at T→θf from above

Experimental: plot 1/χ vs T → straight line with x-intercept at T = θf (Weiss temperature). Note: experimentally θf slightly less than TC (because of spin fluctuations near the transition that are not in mean-field theory).

Origin of the Molecular Field: Exchange Interaction

Why is the Weiss field so large?
From kBTC ≈ μBHmol: TC ~ 1000 K → μBHmol ~ 0.1 eV → Hmol ~ 10⁷ Gauss! But dipole-dipole interaction between neighbouring moments gives only μ₀/a³ ~ 10³ Gauss — far too small. The Weiss field cannot be magnetic in origin.

It is the quantum mechanical exchange interaction, arising from the Pauli exclusion principle and Coulomb repulsion. The Heisenberg exchange Hamiltonian H = −2JexSi·Sj: if Jex > 0, parallel spins (ferromagnet) are favoured; if Jex < 0, antiparallel spins (antiferromagnet). Exchange energies are electrostatic (Coulomb) in origin, scaled by ~0.1–1 eV — exactly right for TC ~ 1000 K.

Slater's Band Model for Fe, Co, Ni

For 3d transition metals, the tight-binding 3d band overlaps with the broad 4s band. Slater proposed that the 3d and 4s electrons share the Fermi level. For Ni (3d⁸4s²: 10 electrons total):

9.4 electrons in 3d band + 0.6 electrons in 4s band

In the 3d band with 5 spin-up slots: 5 − 4.4 = 0.6 μB net moment per atom (experiment: 0.54 μB). For Fe: 5 − 2.8 = 2.2 μB (experiment: 2.22 μB). The agreement is remarkable.

Heisenberg/Heitler–London Exchange

For two interacting electrons (Heitler–London theory), the energy eigenvalues split into singlet and triplet:

E = C ± Jex

where C is the Coulomb integral and Jex is the exchange integral. When Jex > 0 (triplet lower): ferromagnetism. The condition Jex > 0 is met when the Slater integral has the right sign — related to the ratio of interatomic distance to d-orbital radius (Bethe–Slater curve).

Free Electron Theory & Drude Model

Derive the Drude expression for electrical conductivity and state its successes and failures.
Derivation: The Drude model treats conduction electrons as a classical ideal gas. An electron of charge e (magnitude) in electric field E experiences force F = eE. Including a phenomenological resistive damping term due to collisions:
m(dv/dt) = eE − (m/τ)v
where τ is the mean relaxation time between collisions. At steady state (dv/dt = 0): drift velocity vd = eEτ/m. Current density j = nevd = (ne²τ/m)E = σE, giving:
σ = ne²τ/m
Successes: (1) Correctly predicts conductivity magnitude σ ~ 10⁶ Ω⁻¹cm⁻¹ for metals at room T. (2) Correctly predicts Wiedemann–Franz law κ/σT = L₀ (Lorenz number). (3) Hall effect for monovalent metals: RH = −1/ne agrees for Cu, Ag, Au.

Failures: (1) Electronic specific heat: predicts Cve = (3/2)NkB, but experiment gives ~100× smaller value. (2) Positive Hall coefficient in divalent metals (Be, Zn) — implies positive carriers, which classical theory can't explain. (3) Temperature dependence of resistivity at low T: Drude predicts τ → 0, but mean free path becomes very large at low T. (4) Cannot explain insulators — all materials should conduct equally if all valence electrons are free.
What is the Hall effect? Derive the Hall coefficient from the Drude model and explain the sign anomaly.
Setup: Current J flows in ŷ direction, magnetic field B in ẑ direction. The Lorentz force −e(v×B) = evB x̂ pushes electrons in x̂. Positive ions are left behind → an electric field Ex (Hall field) builds up until it balances the Lorentz force.

Drude derivation: In steady state of the Drude equation dp/dt = F − p/τ with F = e[E + v×B]:
Ex = −(1/ne)JyBz = ρHJy  →  RH = ρH/B = −1/ne
Sign anomaly: For Cu, Ag (monovalent) RH ≈ −1.5/ne — close to −1/ne, consistent with electron carriers. But for Be, Zn, Al, RH is positive — suggesting the dominant carriers are positively charged. This cannot be explained by the Drude model.

Band theory explanation: In divalent/trivalent metals, the valence band is nearly full. The dominant current carriers are holes (missing electrons near the top of the valence band). Holes have positive charge and positive effective mass → positive Hall coefficient. The existence of holes is a purely quantum mechanical (band theory) effect.
Explain the Wiedemann–Franz Law and derive the Lorenz number.
The Wiedemann–Franz Law states that for metals, the ratio of thermal conductivity κ to electrical conductivity σ is proportional to temperature:
κ/σT = L₀     (L₀ = Lorenz number ~ 2.45 × 10⁻⁸ W·Ω/K²)
Drude derivation: κ = (1/3)n⟨v²⟩τ·(dE/dT). Using classical equipartition ⟨v²⟩ = 3kBT/m and Cve = (3/2)nkB:
κ = (4/π)(kB/e)²σT  →  L₀Drude = 4kB²/(πe²)
The Drude value overestimates L₀ because Cve is overestimated (classical value) while ⟨v²⟩ is underestimated. The two errors partly cancel, giving a result of the right order.

Sommerfeld result: Using quantum statistics, the correct derivation gives L₀ = π²kB²/(3e²) = 2.45 × 10⁻⁸ W·Ω/K² — in excellent agreement with experiment for most metals.

Physical significance: Both κ and σ are carried by the same electrons (conduction electrons), so their ratio is a universal constant independent of material-specific parameters like n and τ.

Sommerfeld & Fermi Gas

Derive the Fermi energy EF₀ at T=0 and show that the average energy is (3/5)EF₀.
Derivation of EF₀: The 3D density of states (DOS) for a free electron gas:
D(E) = (V/2π²)(2m/ℏ²)3/2√E = C√E
At T=0, all states below EF₀ are filled (f(E) = step function). Total electron number:
N = ∫₀EF₀D(E)dE = (2/3)CEF₀3/2
Solving:
EF₀ = (ℏ²/2m)(3π²N/V)2/3 = (ℏ²/2m)(3π²n)2/3
For Cu: n = 8.5 × 10²² cm⁻³ → EF₀ ≈ 7 eV.

Average energy:
Ū = (1/N)∫₀EF₀E·D(E)dE = (1/N)·C·(2/5)EF₀5/2 = (3/5)EF₀
using N = (2/3)CEF₀3/2. For Cu, Ū ~ 4.2 eV — far larger than the classical kBT ~ 0.026 eV. This enormous "quantum pressure" (Pauli pressure or degeneracy pressure) is what holds up white dwarf stars!
Derive the electronic specific heat using Sommerfeld theory. Why is it much smaller than the Drude prediction?
Physical argument: In the Drude model, all N electrons can be thermally excited → Cve = (3/2)NkB. In Sommerfeld, only electrons within ~kBT of EF can be excited (those above can reach empty states, those far below EF have no empty states to go to). The fraction of "active" electrons ≈ kBT/EF ~ 1/100 at room T.

Quantitative derivation: At finite T, EF(T) ≈ EF₀[1 − (π²/12)(kBT/EF₀)²]. The total internal energy:
U(T) = U₀·[1 + (5π²/12)(kBT/EF)²]
Differentiating (with U₀ = (3/5)NEF₀):
Cve/vol = (π²/2)nkB(kBT/EF) = γT
γ = π²nkB²/(2EF) is the Sommerfeld constant. For Cu, γ ~ 0.7 mJ/mol·K² (theory predicts 0.50, experiment gives 0.695 — the small discrepancy is due to the effective mass m*). The key point: Cve ∝ T, not constant as in Drude.

Band Theory & Kronig–Penney

State and derive Bloch's theorem. What does it physically mean?
Statement: For a Hamiltonian H = −ℏ²∇²/2m + V(r) where V(r) = V(r+R) for all lattice vectors R, the eigenfunctions can be chosen of the form:
ψnk(r) = eik·runk(r)     where unk(r+R) = unk(r)
Proof sketch: Define the translation operator TR: TRψ(r) = ψ(r+R). Since [H, TR] = 0 (H is periodic), H and TR share eigenstates. The eigenvalue of TR must be of modulus 1 (probability conserved): TRψ = eik·Rψ. Writing ψ = eik·ru(r): TRψ = eik·(r+R)u(r+R) = eik·r·eik·R·u(r+R). For this to equal eik·Rψ = eik·Reik·ru(r), we need u(r+R) = u(r). ∎

Physical meaning: (1) Electrons propagate through a perfect crystal without scattering — Bloch states are exact stationary states. (2) The crystal momentum ℏk is conserved. (3) Only deviations from perfect periodicity (phonons, defects) scatter electrons and cause resistance. (4) The electron "sees" all ion cores simultaneously, moving as a renormalised free particle with effective mass m*.
Derive the KP model dispersion relation and explain the appearance of energy gaps.
Starting from the Dirac comb form, the central result is:
(P/αa)sin(αa) + cos(αa) = cos(ka)     α² = 2mE/ℏ²
Why gaps appear: The left-hand side (LHS) is bounded only by |LHS| ≤ 1 for solutions to exist in terms of a real crystal momentum k. When the LHS exceeds ±1, there are no real k solutions → these are forbidden energies → energy gaps.

Location of gaps: Gaps occur at αa = nπ, i.e., k = nπ/a — the Brillouin zone boundaries. At these points, the electron wavelength λ = 2π/k = 2a/n satisfies the Bragg condition for reflection from the lattice: 2a sin θ = nλ with θ = 90° gives 2a = nλ. The Bragg-reflected waves combine to form two standing waves ψ₊ ∝ cos(nπx/a) (maximum probability at ion sites, lower PE, lower energy) and ψ₋ ∝ sin(nπx/a) (maximum probability between ions, higher PE, higher energy). The energy difference between these two standing waves is the gap.
What is effective mass? Derive the expression and explain negative effective mass and holes.
Derivation: Group velocity of a Bloch electron: v = (1/ℏ)(dE/dk). In applied field E: F = ℏ(dk/dt). Acceleration:
a = dv/dt = (1/ℏ)(d²E/dk²)(dk/dt) = (1/ℏ²)(d²E/dk²)F
Comparing F = m*a:
m* = ℏ²/(d²E/dk²)
Near band bottom (k ≈ 0): E ≈ E₀ + ℏ²k²/2m* with m* > 0. Electron accelerates normally.

Near band top (k ≈ π/a): Band curves downward → d²E/dk² < 0 → m* < 0. In a field, these electrons accelerate opposite to the force — they decelerate! This violates classical intuition and is a pure quantum effect.

Holes: A nearly-full band (e.g., valence band of a semiconductor with one electron removed) is more usefully described by the hole — the missing electron. The hole has: charge +e, mass mh* = −me* > 0, wavevector kh = −ke. The hole behaves as a real positive carrier in transport and Hall experiments. It is the quantum mechanical origin of p-type semiconductors and the positive Hall coefficient in divalent metals.

Magnetism

Derive the Langevin theory of paramagnetism and obtain the Curie law.
Setup: n dipoles per unit volume, each with moment μ. In field H, potential energy E = −μH cos θ. The number of dipoles in solid angle dΩ = 2π sin θ dθ:
dn = k·eμH cosθ/kBT·2π sinθ dθ
Magnetisation M = ∫μ cosθ dn / ∫dn. Let a = μH/kBT, x = cosθ:
M = nμ·L(a)    where    L(a) = coth(a) − 1/a   (Langevin function)
Curie Law (small a, ordinary T): Expand L(a) ≈ a/3 for a ≪ 1:
M ≈ nμ·(a/3) = nμ²H/(3kBT)  →  χ = M/H = nμ²/(3kBT) = C/T
Saturation (large a, low T/high H): L(a) → 1, M → nμ = Ms. All moments perfectly aligned.

Limitation: The classical theory assumes continuous orientation. Quantum theory uses discrete mJ values → Brillouin function. For J = 1/2 (pure spin): B1/2(a) = tanh(a). The quantum result generally matches experiment better.
Derive the Curie–Weiss law using the Weiss molecular field theory and find an expression for TC.
Weiss hypothesis: In addition to the external field H, each atom feels an effective internal field Heff = H + λM, where λ is the Weiss constant (dimensionless parameter representing the strength of the exchange interaction).

For T > TC (paramagnetic region, small M): Using the classical Curie result M = C·Heff/T = C(H + λM)/T:
M(T − Cλ) = CH  →  χ = M/H = C/(T − Cλ) = C/(T − θf)
where θf = Cλ is the paramagnetic Curie temperature. This is the Curie–Weiss Law. χ → ∞ as T → θf from above — the susceptibility diverges at the ordering transition.

Finding TC: Below TC, spontaneous M exists for H=0. From M = MsBJ(gJμBλM/kBT) and the small-a expansion of BJ: the condition for a non-trivial solution is:
kBTC = nλg²J(J+1)μB²/3 = nλμ²/3  →  θf = TC
In practice, experimentally θf < TC because mean-field theory overestimates the stability of the ordered state (short-range correlations above TC that are ignored in Weiss theory).
Why do iron group transition metals not obey the gJ formula for magnetic moment? Explain orbital quenching.
The problem: Rare earth ions follow Peff = gJ√[J(J+1)] beautifully. But iron group (3d) ions are well described by Peff = 2√[S(S+1)] (spin only), i.e., as if L = 0.

Why? In a crystal, the ion is surrounded by ligand atoms or anions (crystal field). The non-uniform electric field of these ligands breaks spherical symmetry. The orbital angular momentum states (d-orbitals) are no longer degenerate — they split and hybridise with the lattice. The orbital angular momentum L can no longer precess freely about the magnetic field direction: it is "locked in" to the crystal axes. Quantum mechanically, the expectation value ⟨L⟩ = 0 in the eigenstates of the crystal-field Hamiltonian (which is real → ψ* = ψ → only real combinations of Ylm survive → ⟨L⟩ = Im⟨r×p⟩ = 0). This phenomenon is called orbital quenching.

Why rare earths don't quench: The 4f shell is deep inside the atom, shielded by the outer 5s²5p⁶ electrons. The crystal field reaches the 4f shell only weakly — much weaker than the spin-orbit coupling. Hence L-S coupling (Hund's rules + J = L+S coupling) remains valid, and the Landé g formula works.
What is the exchange interaction? Why can't dipole-dipole interaction explain ferromagnetism?
Dipole-dipole failure: The energy of two magnetic dipoles separated by distance a is Edip ~ μ₀μ²/a³. For μ ~ μB = 10⁻²³ J/T and a ~ 2 Å: Edip ~ 10⁻²³ T·m³/A × (9.27×10⁻²⁴)²/(10⁻³⁰) ~ 10⁻²⁵ J ~ 10⁻⁶ eV. This corresponds to a temperature of only ~10⁻³ K — far below TC ~ 1000 K for Fe. The dipolar interaction is ~10⁶ times too small.

Exchange interaction: Arises from two quantum mechanical effects combined: (1) Coulomb repulsion between electrons (energy scale ~ eV), and (2) the Pauli exclusion principle (antisymmetry of the two-electron wavefunction). When two atoms come close, their electron wavefunctions overlap. The Heitler-London calculation gives:
Hex = −2JexS1·S2
where Jex = ∫ψa*(1)ψb*(2)(e²/r₁₂)ψa(2)ψb(1)dV is the exchange integral. If Jex > 0: triplet (parallel spins) lower in energy → ferromagnetism. This is an electrostatic energy (~0.1 eV) which correctly gives TC ~ 1000 K. The "exchange field" felt by a spin is the many-body effect of all its neighbours — this is the Weiss molecular field.
